# Section 1: Anatomy of an Engagement Ring

### The Two Halves

Every engagement ring, regardless of its style or price, is composed of two fundamental elements: the stone and the structure that holds it. Understanding each component -- and how they interact -- is the foundation for making an informed purchase.

The stone is the centrepiece. In the vast majority of engagement rings, this is a diamond, though coloured gemstones have their own set of considerations. The structure encompasses everything else: the setting that grips the stone, the basket or gallery beneath it, and the band that wraps around the finger. Each of these components has measurable qualities that affect durability, appearance, and long-term wearability.

Too often, buyers fixate entirely on the diamond and treat the metalwork as an afterthought. This is a mistake. A poorly constructed setting can damage an exceptional diamond. A band that is too thin will warp within months. The ring is a system, and every part of that system matters.

### The Diamond: A Brief Overview of the 4Cs

The 4Cs -- Cut, Colour, Clarity, and Carat weight -- are the standardised framework for evaluating diamonds. We will address Cut in considerable depth in Section 3 of this guide, so here we provide only an orientation.

**Cut** refers to how well a diamond has been faceted and polished relative to ideal geometric proportions. It is the single most important factor in determining how a diamond looks to the naked eye. A well-cut diamond returns light efficiently, creating the brilliance and sparkle that people associate with fine diamonds. Cut is graded from Excellent to Poor by the Gemological Institute of America (GIA).

**Colour** grades how close a diamond is to being completely colourless. The GIA scale runs from D (colourless) to Z (light yellow or brown). Most engagement ring diamonds fall in the D to J range. Differences between adjacent grades -- say, E versus F -- are extremely subtle and typically invisible when the stone is mounted. Below approximately J colour, warmth becomes noticeable in most settings, though this is partly a matter of personal preference and partly a function of the metal colour chosen.

**Clarity** measures the presence of internal inclusions and surface blemishes. The GIA scale runs from Flawless (FL) down through Included (I1, I2, I3). For practical purposes, any diamond graded SI1 (Slightly Included 1) or above is likely to appear clean to the unaided eye, though this varies with diamond size, shape, and the nature and location of specific inclusions. An SI1 with a white feather near the edge will look very different from an SI1 with a dark crystal directly beneath the table facet.

**Carat** is a unit of weight, not size. One carat equals 0.200 grams. Two diamonds of identical carat weight can have noticeably different face-up dimensions depending on how they are cut -- a point we will examine in detail in Section 3.

#### Grading Certificates and Reliable Laboratories

A grading certificate (more accurately called a grading report) is an independent assessment of a diamond's characteristics. Not all laboratories grade to the same standard, and this is a critical point for consumers to understand.

The **Gemological Institute of America (GIA)** is widely regarded as the most consistent and stringent grading laboratory in the world. Their standards, particularly for colour and clarity, are well-calibrated and reproducible. A GIA-graded G colour diamond will reliably look like a G colour diamond.

The **International Gemological Institute (IGI)** has become the dominant laboratory for grading lab-grown diamonds. Their grading standards for lab-grown stones are generally consistent and reliable. For natural diamonds, IGI grading has historically been considered slightly more lenient than GIA in some categories, though the practical significance of this varies.

The **American Gem Society (AGS)** is particularly respected for its cut grading system, which uses a numerical 0-to-10 scale and includes light performance analysis. AGS Ideal (grade 0) for cut is a meaningful distinction.

Laboratories such as EGL, GSI, and various regional labs often grade more leniently than GIA. A diamond graded F colour by one of these labs might grade as G or H at GIA. This does not make the diamond worse -- the stone is what it is -- but it means you may be paying F-colour prices for what is objectively a G-colour stone. When comparing diamonds, ensure you are comparing certificates from the same laboratory, or adjust your expectations accordingly.

### The Setting and Head

The setting, sometimes called the head, is the metalwork that directly holds the diamond in place. It is the most structurally critical component of the ring, and differences in setting design have real consequences for both the security of the stone and the visual appearance of the finished piece.

#### Prong (Claw) Setting

The prong setting is the most common type for engagement rings. Individual metal prongs extend upward from a base and grip the diamond at its girdle (the widest point). This design maximises the amount of light that can enter and exit the diamond, which is why it remains the default choice for solitaires and many other styles.

Prong settings are available in four-prong and six-prong configurations, each with distinct characteristics we will discuss shortly. The trade-off with prong settings is that the stone is more exposed than in other setting types. Prongs can catch on fabrics, and if a prong is bent or worn, the stone is at risk of loosening.

#### Bezel Setting

A bezel setting encircles the diamond with a thin band of metal that wraps around the entire girdle. This provides excellent security -- it is very difficult to lose a stone from a properly executed bezel -- and creates a sleek, modern profile. The trade-off is that the metal rim obscures a small amount of the diamond's perimeter, and less light enters from the sides. Bezels are an excellent choice for people who work with their hands, exercise frequently, or simply prefer a lower-profile ring.

A partial bezel, sometimes called a semi-bezel or half-bezel, covers only portions of the girdle, offering a compromise between the security of a full bezel and the light access of a prong setting.

#### Tension Setting

In a tension setting, the diamond appears to float between the two ends of the band, held in place by the spring pressure of the metal itself. The effect is visually striking, but there are practical considerations. True tension settings require precise engineering -- the metal must exert exactly the right amount of pressure on the stone, and the diamond must be cut to exact specifications. They are more difficult to resize, and not all jewellers are comfortable working on them. If you choose a tension setting, ensure it is made by a manufacturer with specific expertise in this design.

#### Channel Setting

Channel settings seat diamonds in a groove cut into the metal, with the stones held in place by the upper edges of the channel walls. This is most commonly used for accent diamonds along the band rather than for a centre stone. Well-executed channel setting creates a smooth, flush surface with no prongs to catch. The risk with channel settings is that poorly cut channels can allow stones to shift or fall out, and repairs can be complex.

#### Pave Setting

Pave (from the French word for "paved") setting covers surfaces of the ring with small diamonds, typically ranging from 0.8mm to 2.0mm in diameter. Each small diamond is held by tiny prongs or beads of metal raised from the surface. Micro-pave uses even smaller diamonds, often below 1.0mm.

Pave adds significant sparkle and visual impact, but it also adds maintenance considerations. Small pave diamonds can and do fall out over time, particularly in areas subject to wear such as the sides and underside of the band. Quality of pave setting varies enormously -- this is discussed in detail in Section 2.

#### Halo Setting

A halo surrounds the centre diamond with a ring of smaller diamonds. This design makes the centre stone appear larger and adds overall brilliance. Halos can be single or double (two concentric rings of small diamonds). The visual size increase is genuine -- a 1.00ct round diamond in a halo setting will present a total diamond cluster that appears similar in diameter to a 1.50ct stone, depending on the size of the halo diamonds.

The practical consideration with halos is that the small surrounding diamonds are pave-set and subject to the same maintenance considerations as any pave work. A well-made halo from a quality manufacturer will hold up well; a poorly made one will shed stones regularly.

### The Prongs: Details That Matter

#### Four Prongs vs Six Prongs

A four-prong setting allows more of the diamond to be visible and lets more light reach the stone. The diamond's outline is more apparent, and the overall look is slightly more open. However, four prongs provide less security. If one prong fails, the stone is held by only three points and is at meaningful risk of loss.

A six-prong setting provides greater security -- even if one prong fails, five remain to hold the stone. Six prongs also create a slightly rounder appearance when viewed from above, which can be desirable on round brilliant diamonds. The trade-off is that more of the diamond's perimeter is obscured by metal.

For round diamonds above 1.00ct, we generally see six-prong settings as the more prudent choice. For smaller rounds, or for fancy shapes where prong placement is dictated by the stone's geometry, four prongs are standard and perfectly adequate when well-constructed and regularly inspected.

#### Prong Shapes

**Round prongs** are the most common. They present a small circular cross-section where they contact the diamond and are relatively easy to set and re-tip. They offer a classic, unobtrusive appearance.

**Pointed or claw prongs** taper to a fine point at the tip. These are more decorative and can create an elegant, vintage-inspired look. The thinner tip means less metal is visible from the top, but pointed prongs are slightly more susceptible to catching on fabrics and can wear down more quickly at the tip.

**Flat or tab prongs** are wider and flatter, distributing their contact with the diamond over a larger area. They are sturdy and provide excellent grip, but they cover more of the diamond's surface. Tab prongs are often used on emerald-cut and other step-cut diamonds.

**V-prongs** are shaped like a V and are specifically designed for diamonds with pointed corners -- princess cuts, marquise, pear shapes, and kite-shaped stones. The V cradles the vulnerable point of the diamond, protecting it from chipping. Any pointed diamond should be set with V-prongs at its points; a round prong on a princess-cut corner is both visually awkward and structurally inadequate.

#### How Prongs Grip the Diamond

A properly set prong grips the diamond at its girdle -- the narrow edge that separates the crown (top) from the pavilion (bottom). The jeweller cuts a small notch into each prong called a **prong seat** or **bearing**. This notch is shaped to match the curvature of the diamond's girdle at that exact point.

The precision of the prong seat is critical. If the seat is too shallow, the diamond sits too high and the prong's grip is weak. If it is too deep, the prong may apply excessive pressure to the girdle, potentially causing damage over time, and the diamond sits too low. The seat must be cut at exactly the right height so the diamond's girdle sits level and the prong tips can be bent smoothly over the crown facets to secure the stone.

When you look at a well-set diamond under magnification, you should see each prong contacting the diamond cleanly, with the prong seat cradling the girdle snugly and the prong tip folded neatly over the edge of the crown. There should be no visible gaps between the prong and the diamond, and the stone should not rock or shift when gently probed.

### The Basket and Gallery

The basket (sometimes called the gallery) is the structure beneath the diamond that connects the prongs to the band. In many settings, the prongs rise from a ring or cradle of metal that sits below the diamond, and this assembly is what we refer to as the basket.

#### Cathedral vs Basket Settings

In a **cathedral setting**, the metal from the band sweeps upward in graceful arches to meet the head, somewhat resembling the flying buttresses of a cathedral. This design raises the diamond higher above the finger and provides strong structural support. Cathedral settings are generally very secure and create an elegant profile view.

In a standard **basket setting**, the prongs rise from a simpler structure that does not integrate as dramatically with the band. The basket may be a flat platform, a series of bridges, or a wire framework. Basket settings tend to sit lower on the finger and can be more compact in profile.

#### Light Performance and the Basket

The design of the basket directly affects how much light reaches the diamond from below and the sides. An open basket with minimal metal beneath the diamond allows light to pass through the pavilion freely, contributing to the stone's brilliance. A heavy or closed basket blocks light from entering through the lower portion of the diamond, which can make the stone appear darker than its cut would otherwise produce.

This is why, when evaluating a ring, you should look at it from the side and underneath, not just from above. A diamond that appears lively and brilliant in a tweezers may look noticeably different once set in a heavy, closed basket.

### The Band (Shank)

The band -- technically called the shank -- is the part of the ring that encircles the finger. Its dimensions and profile have a direct impact on comfort, durability, and the visual proportionality of the finished ring.

#### Width and Thickness

Band width is measured across the top surface (the dimension you see looking at the ring from above), while thickness is measured from the inner surface to the outer surface (the dimension you see looking at the ring in profile).

For solitaire engagement rings, band widths typically range from 1.6mm to 3.0mm. A 2.0mm width is the most common standard. Widths below 1.6mm begin to compromise structural integrity and feel fragile on the finger. Wider bands (2.5mm to 3.0mm) create a bolder look and provide more structural rigidity, but they must be proportionate to the centre stone -- a very wide band with a small diamond can look unbalanced.

Band thickness is arguably more important than width from a durability standpoint. We recommend a minimum thickness of 1.6mm for any engagement ring, with 1.8mm to 2.0mm being preferable. Rings thinner than 1.6mm are prone to bending and warping with daily wear, particularly in softer metals like 18-karat gold. A ring that warps loses its round shape, which affects fit, comfort, and the security of the setting.

#### Band Profiles

**Comfort fit** bands have a slightly domed interior surface that reduces contact with the finger, making the ring easier to slide on and off and more comfortable for extended wear. Most modern engagement rings use comfort fit profiles, and we recommend them as the default choice.

**Flat (or standard) fit** bands have a flat interior surface. They feel slightly tighter than comfort fit at the same nominal size and can create more friction during removal. Some people prefer the feel, but comfort fit is generally superior for rings worn daily.

**Knife edge** bands taper to a ridge along the outer surface, creating a slim, angular profile. They are visually distinctive but the thin edge can feel sharp against adjacent fingers and is more susceptible to denting.

**Split shank** bands divide into two or more strands as they approach the setting. This creates a dramatic, contemporary look and can make the centre stone appear larger by providing visual contrast. Split shanks require careful engineering at the point where the band divides, as this junction is a potential weak point.

**Rounded (or D-profile)** bands have a gently domed exterior and a flat or slightly curved interior. They are traditional and comfortable, and the curved exterior sheds light attractively.

#### Proportionality

The band should be proportionate to the centre stone. As a rough guideline, a diamond below 0.50ct suits a band of 1.6mm to 2.0mm width. A diamond of 1.00ct to 1.50ct pairs well with 1.8mm to 2.5mm. Larger diamonds of 2.00ct and above can carry bands of 2.5mm to 3.0mm or wider. These are guidelines, not rules -- personal preference and finger size also play important roles.

### The Metal

#### Gold Karats: 18k vs 14k

The karat system measures the proportion of pure gold in an alloy. Pure gold is 24 karats (24k) but is far too soft for jewellery that will be worn daily.

**18-karat gold** is 75% pure gold and 25% alloying metals. It has a rich, warm colour (in yellow gold), good durability, and is the standard for fine jewellery in Australia, Europe, and much of the world. It is more resistant to tarnish than 14k and has a slightly heavier, more substantial feel.

**14-karat gold** is 58.3% pure gold and 41.7% alloying metals. The higher proportion of alloy metals makes it harder and more scratch-resistant than 18k, which is a genuine advantage for rings subject to heavy daily wear. The colour is slightly less saturated than 18k, though the difference is subtle in yellow gold and essentially invisible in white gold. 14k is the standard in the United States and is a perfectly respectable choice.

The practical differences between 18k and 14k are modest. 18k is marginally softer and richer in colour. 14k is marginally harder and more affordable. Neither is objectively "better" -- it is a matter of preference and budget.

#### Platinum

Platinum is denser than gold (approximately 60% heavier by volume), naturally white, and does not require rhodium plating. It is hypoallergenic and extremely durable in the sense that metal is displaced rather than lost when platinum is scratched. Over time, platinum develops a distinctive patina -- a soft, satiny surface that many people find attractive. It can be polished back to a high shine at any time.

The primary consideration with platinum is that it scratches more visibly than rhodium-plated white gold in the short term. However, platinum scratches do not remove metal (it is merely displaced), whereas gold scratches result in actual metal loss. Over a lifetime, a platinum ring retains more of its original mass than a gold ring.

#### White Gold and Rhodium Plating

White gold is created by alloying yellow gold with white metals, typically palladium, nickel, or a combination thereof. The resulting alloy is not truly white -- it has a warm, slightly yellowish or greyish tone. To achieve the bright white appearance that most consumers expect, white gold jewellery is electroplated with rhodium, a platinum-group metal that is extremely hard and brilliantly white.

The important thing to understand about rhodium plating is that it wears off. Depending on the wearer's body chemistry, level of activity, and how often the ring contacts hard surfaces, rhodium plating typically lasts six months to two years before the underlying warm tone of the white gold begins to show through. Re-plating is a routine procedure that any jeweller can perform, usually for a modest fee, but it is a recurring maintenance requirement.

If the idea of periodic re-plating is unappealing, platinum or palladium are naturally white alternatives that do not require plating.

#### Rose Gold

Rose gold gets its warm, pinkish hue from a higher proportion of copper in the alloy. A typical 18k rose gold alloy is 75% gold, approximately 22.5% copper, and a small percentage of silver. The copper content makes rose gold slightly harder than yellow gold of the same karat. It does not require plating and its colour is stable over time. One consideration is that copper content can cause allergic reactions in individuals with copper sensitivities, though this is uncommon.

### How All Components Work Together

An engagement ring is a system, and the quality of the whole depends on the quality of each component and how well they are integrated. The diamond's cut determines how it handles light, but the setting determines how much light reaches the diamond. The prongs secure the stone, but their placement affects visual presentation and wearability. The basket influences brightness. The band supports everything above it -- if it is too thin, it will flex and compromise the setting. The metal must be appropriate for the design: a delicate micro-pave band requires the hardness of 14k gold or platinum, not the relative softness of 18k yellow gold.

When evaluating a ring, resist the temptation to assess any component in isolation. A truly well-made engagement ring is one in which every component has been selected and executed with the same level of care. This is what separates fine jewellery from mass-produced pieces.

# Section 2: Craftsmanship Methods and Inspection Guide

### Why Craftsmanship Matters

Two rings can use identical diamonds, identical metals, and identical designs yet differ enormously in quality. The difference lies in craftsmanship -- the precision, skill, and care applied during manufacturing and finishing. Craftsmanship defects are not always visible to the casual observer, but they have real consequences: stones that loosen and fall out, bands that warp, surfaces that dull prematurely, and prongs that catch and snag on everything they contact.

This section provides a systematic approach to inspecting an engagement ring's construction. You do not need specialised tools to perform most of these checks -- a standard 10x jeweller's loupe and good lighting are sufficient. If you do not own a loupe, any reputable jeweller will lend you one or allow you to use theirs. If a seller discourages you from examining a piece closely, that alone tells you something.

### Prong Inspection

Prongs are the first line of defence for your diamond. They should be inspected carefully before purchase and periodically throughout the ring's life.

#### Symmetry and Spacing

All prongs should be evenly spaced around the diamond. In a four-prong setting, the prongs should be at 90-degree intervals. In a six-prong setting, they should be at 60-degree intervals. Uneven spacing is both visually distracting and structurally suboptimal -- it creates unequal distribution of stress on the diamond's girdle.

Look at the ring from directly above. The prongs should appear as mirror images of each other across the centre of the stone. If one prong is noticeably closer to its neighbour than the others, or if the prongs are not aligned symmetrically relative to the band, the setting work was not performed to a high standard.

#### Height and Thickness

All prongs should be the same height. Uneven prong heights mean that one or more prongs are not making full contact with the diamond, which compromises security. You can check this by viewing the ring in profile -- the prong tips should form a level plane around the diamond.

Prong thickness should be consistent and appropriate for the stone size. Prongs that are too thin will wear down quickly and may not withstand a knock. Prongs that are too thick obscure excessive amounts of the diamond and look heavy. For a round diamond in the 0.50ct to 1.50ct range, prong tips of approximately 0.8mm to 1.2mm width are typical.

#### Common Prong Defects

**Overcut prong seats**: If the jeweller has cut the prong seat too deeply, the prong may be weakened at precisely the point where it needs to be strongest. Look for prongs that appear noticeably thinned or narrowed where they contact the diamond.

**Poorly finished tips**: Prong tips should be smooth, rounded, and polished. Flat, rough, or sharp prong tips catch on fabrics and hair and indicate careless finishing. Run your finger gently over the prong tips -- you should feel a smooth, rounded surface with no edges or burrs.

**Bent or misaligned prongs**: Each prong should follow a straight, clean line from its base to its tip. Prongs that curve, lean, or deviate from their intended path suggest either poor setting technique or damage from wear.

**Uneven pressure**: Each prong should contact the diamond with equal pressure. A prong that sits slightly away from the stone (visible as a gap between prong and diamond) is not doing its job. Conversely, a prong that is pressing too hard may be placing undue stress on the girdle.

#### Re-tipping

Over time, prong tips wear down through contact with surfaces during daily activities. When prong tips become noticeably thin, they need to be **re-tipped** -- a process in which a jeweller adds a small amount of metal to the worn tip and reshapes it. This is routine maintenance, not a sign of a defective ring. However, the frequency with which re-tipping is needed is influenced by the original prong thickness, the metal hardness, and the wearer's activity level.

Platinum prongs require re-tipping less frequently than gold prongs because platinum displaces rather than loses metal when abraded. 14k gold prongs last longer than 18k gold prongs due to their greater hardness.

We recommend having prong condition checked by a jeweller every 12 months, or sooner if you notice a prong that feels rough or appears thin.

### Diamond Seating

How a diamond sits in its setting is a fundamental indicator of craftsmanship quality.

#### Level Seating

The diamond should sit perfectly level in the setting -- that is, the table facet (the large flat facet on top) should be parallel to the plane of the band. A diamond that tilts to one side indicates uneven prong seats or a poorly shaped basket. Tilted diamonds look wrong, even to an untrained eye, and they are structurally compromised because the prong pressure is unevenly distributed.

To check, view the ring in profile from multiple angles. The top of the diamond should appear level relative to the band from every direction.

#### Pressure Distribution

Each prong should exert approximately equal pressure on the diamond. You can check this, very carefully, by using a fine-pointed wooden tool (never metal) to gently nudge the diamond from different directions. The stone should not move, shift, or rock in its setting. Any movement indicates inadequate seating.

Please note: do not perform this test aggressively. The point is to verify that the stone is secure, not to force it loose. A gentle touch is all that is needed. If you are uncomfortable performing this check, ask a jeweller to do it.

#### Gap Checking

Using a loupe, examine the junction between each prong and the diamond. There should be no visible gap between the inner face of the prong and the diamond's surface. Gaps indicate that the prong seat was not cut precisely or that the prong has not been fully tightened against the stone.

Small gaps may seem insignificant, but they allow the diamond to shift microscopically with each impact, which gradually widens the gap over time. A stone that is perfectly secure today may be loose in six months if the initial seating was imprecise.

#### Rocking Stones

A diamond that rocks in its setting -- pivoting slightly when pressed on one side -- is a serious defect. Rocking indicates that the prong seats are not at a consistent height, or that fewer than the intended number of prongs are actually making contact with the stone. A rocking diamond will eventually become a lost diamond. This should be identified and corrected before purchase or, if discovered after purchase, addressed immediately by a qualified jeweller.

### Pave and Micro-Pave Quality

Pave setting is one of the most skill-dependent aspects of ring manufacturing, and it is where the gap between excellent and mediocre craftsmanship is most apparent.

#### What Good Pave Looks Like

In high-quality pave work, the small diamonds should be:

- **Evenly spaced**: The gaps between stones should be consistent. The metalwork between diamonds should be uniform in width -- typically 0.1mm to 0.2mm of metal visible between stones.
- **Level**: All pave diamonds should sit at the same height. Run your finger across a pave surface -- it should feel smooth, almost like a continuous surface. Stones that sit high or low disrupt both the visual uniformity and the tactile smoothness.
- **Securely held**: Each small diamond should be firmly gripped by its prongs or beads. No stone should wiggle or rotate when probed with a fine point.
- **Clean**: The metal between and around the pave diamonds should be neatly finished, with no rough spots, tool marks, or excess metal.

#### Common Pave Defects

**Uneven stone heights**: Diamonds that sit at different heights create a bumpy surface and catch on fabrics. This is the most common pave defect and indicates either rushed work or insufficient skill.

**Irregular spacing**: Gaps that vary noticeably from one stone to the next suggest poor planning or execution. In a well-laid pave pattern, the regularity should be immediately apparent.

**Visible drill marks or tool marks**: During the pave process, the setter uses burrs and gravers to create seats and raise prongs. In finished work, the marks from these tools should be cleaned up and polished away. Visible tool marks in the metal between pave stones indicate incomplete finishing.

**Weak or missing prongs**: Each pave diamond is held by small beads or prongs of metal. If these are thin, irregular, or missing entirely on one side of a stone, that stone is at risk of falling out. Examine the beads under magnification -- they should be uniform, well-formed, and in full contact with the diamonds they are holding.

#### Shared vs Individual Prongs

In some pave configurations, a single bead or prong holds two adjacent diamonds simultaneously (a shared prong). This is standard practice and, when well-executed, is perfectly secure. However, shared prongs mean that if one stone falls out, its neighbour may also be compromised because the shared prong is no longer under balanced pressure. Individual prongs (where each stone has its own dedicated prongs) offer slightly greater security but require more metal and skill.

### Casting Quality

The majority of engagement rings are manufactured through a process called lost-wax casting, in which a wax model of the ring is encased in plaster, the wax is burned out, and molten metal is poured into the resulting cavity. Casting quality varies significantly and has direct implications for the ring's structural integrity.

#### Porosity

Porosity -- small holes or voids within the metal -- is the most common casting defect. Porosity occurs when gas bubbles become trapped in the metal during solidification.

**Surface porosity** appears as tiny pits or holes on the surface of the ring. Minor surface porosity can sometimes be polished out or filled with laser welding, but extensive surface pitting indicates a fundamentally flawed casting. Examine the ring's surface under magnification, particularly in recessed areas and on the inside of the band where finishing is sometimes less thorough.

**Internal (subsurface) porosity** is not visible from the outside but weakens the metal from within. It can cause the ring to crack or break under stress. Internal porosity is difficult to detect without destructive testing, which is why purchasing from reputable manufacturers who maintain rigorous quality control is important.

#### Vacuum Casting

High-quality manufacturers use vacuum-assisted or centrifugal casting to minimise porosity. In vacuum casting, the air is evacuated from the flask before the metal is introduced, dramatically reducing the occurrence of gas bubbles. The difference in quality between vacuum-cast and gravity-cast jewellery is significant and well-documented.

#### Tree Marks

During casting, multiple rings are typically cast simultaneously on a branching structure called a casting tree. Each ring is connected to the tree by a small channel called a sprue. After casting, the rings are cut from the tree and the sprue attachment point is filed and polished away.

In well-finished pieces, the sprue point should be completely invisible. In poorly finished pieces, you may see a small flat spot, bump, or discoloured area -- usually on the inside of the band -- where the sprue was attached. This is a minor defect but indicative of insufficient finishing attention.

### Handmade Jewellery vs Cast Jewellery

The previous section focused on casting quality because casting is, by a wide margin, the most common method used to produce engagement rings today. But it is not the only method. Some jewellers build rings entirely by hand from solid metal stock -- a process known as hand fabrication. Understanding the difference between these two approaches helps you ask better questions and evaluate what you are being shown.

#### Cast Jewellery

As described above, cast jewellery is produced by pouring molten metal into a mould. The mould is created from a wax or resin model of the ring, which is itself often produced using CAD (computer-aided design) and 3D printing. This method is efficient, repeatable, and allows for highly complex and intricate designs that would be extremely difficult or impossible to produce by hand -- detailed filigree work, elaborate halo settings, and ornate gallery designs, for example.

The quality of cast jewellery depends almost entirely on the casting process itself. When the temperatures are precisely controlled, the vacuum is properly drawn, the alloy composition is correct, and the finishing is thorough, cast jewellery can be excellent. A well-cast ring with dense, porosity-free metal and careful hand-finishing will serve its wearer reliably for decades.

The critical point, covered in detail above, is that not all casting is equal. A ring that has been cast under poor conditions -- without vacuum assistance, at incorrect temperatures, or with an inferior alloy -- will contain internal weaknesses that may not be apparent at the time of purchase but will reveal themselves over years of wear.

#### Handmade (Hand-Fabricated) Jewellery

Hand-fabricated rings are built by a jeweller working directly with solid metal stock: wire, sheet, tubing, and bar. Rather than pouring metal into a mould, the jeweller shapes the ring through a series of manual and mechanical processes:

- **Forging** -- hammering or pressing the metal to shape it and refine its grain structure
- **Bending** -- shaping wire or sheet metal around mandrels and forming tools
- **Soldering** -- joining separate metal components together using a torch and solder alloy
- **Machining** -- using lathes, mills, or other precision tools to achieve exact dimensions
- **Hand finishing** -- filing, sanding, and polishing every surface by hand to the final standard

One of the structural advantages of hand fabrication is that working the metal -- particularly forging -- compresses and aligns its crystalline grain structure. This process, sometimes called work-hardening, can increase the metal's density and strength compared to cast metal of the same alloy. A forged band, for instance, tends to be harder and more resistant to bending than a cast band of identical dimensions and karat.

However, hand fabrication has practical limitations. It is significantly more labour-intensive than casting, which means it costs more -- often considerably more -- for an equivalent design. Not every design lends itself to fabrication; highly complex or sculptural forms that are straightforward to cast may be impractical or impossible to build from solid stock. And hand fabrication requires a high level of skill. Poorly executed fabrication -- uneven soldering, inconsistent thickness, rough filing -- can produce results worse than a well-managed casting process.

#### A Balanced View

It would be misleading to declare one method universally superior to the other. The reality is more nuanced:

- Hand-fabricated jewellery can offer superior metal density and structural strength, particularly in simple band designs where the forging process provides a genuine material advantage.
- High-quality cast jewellery, produced under controlled conditions with proper finishing, can be equally durable and allows for a far wider range of design possibilities.
- The craftsmanship and engineering applied to the ring matter more than the production method alone. A beautifully cast ring from a skilled manufacturer will outperform a poorly fabricated ring from an unskilled bench jeweller, and vice versa.

The following table summarises the key differences:

| Feature | Cast Jewellery | Handmade Jewellery |
|---|---|---|
| Production method | Molten metal poured into a mould | Built from solid metal stock |
| Metal density | Depends on casting quality | Often increased through forging |
| Design flexibility | Very high | More limited |
| Labour required | Lower | Much higher |
| Typical cost | Lower | Higher |

Most high-quality engagement rings sold today are cast, and there is nothing wrong with that. What truly matters is dense casting, proper alloy control, precise setting work, and thoughtful ring engineering. A well-cast ring that ticks all of those boxes will last a lifetime.

That said, it is worth asking your jeweller how their rings are made and finished. Not because one answer is right and another is wrong, but because the answer tells you something about the jeweller's process, their attention to detail, and whether they understand the material properties of what they are selling. A jeweller who can explain their production method clearly -- and why they have chosen it -- is one who takes their craft seriously.

### Basket and Gallery Inspection

The basket or gallery is the structural framework beneath the diamond. It is often overlooked during casual inspection because it is not readily visible when the ring is worn, but its quality directly affects both the ring's strength and the diamond's light performance.

#### Symmetry

View the ring from underneath and from the sides. The basket should be symmetrical -- the metal framework should be evenly balanced on all sides. Asymmetrical baskets indicate misalignment during manufacturing and can result in the diamond sitting off-centre or at a slight tilt.

#### Finishing

The inner surfaces of the basket should be polished and free of rough spots, tool marks, and sharp edges. Sharp edges inside the basket can scratch the skin if the ring is handled from below, and they indicate a lack of finishing care. Run a fingertip gently along the inside surfaces of the basket -- nothing should feel rough or catch on your skin.

#### Structural Integrity

The junctions where basket components meet the prongs and where the basket connects to the band should be clean, solid, and free of visible solder lines or gaps. These junctions are stress points, and any weakness here can lead to structural failure. Under magnification, the metal at these junctions should flow smoothly, without visible seams or voids.

### Band Thickness and Dimensions

#### How to Measure

Band thickness and width can be measured with a millimetre-graduated calliper, which is an inexpensive tool available from any hardware store. Measure the band at its thinnest point, which is usually at the bottom (the 6 o'clock position) or at the sides (the 3 and 9 o'clock positions).

#### Minimum Dimensions

For a plain solitaire engagement ring intended for daily wear:

- **Minimum band thickness**: 1.6mm. Below this, the ring is susceptible to bending and warping. We consider 1.6mm an absolute minimum, and 1.8mm to 2.0mm is preferable.
- **Minimum band width**: 1.6mm for a delicate look, 2.0mm to 2.5mm for a standard proportion.

For rings with pave-set diamonds in the band, slightly greater dimensions are needed because the diamond seats reduce the effective cross-section of the metal:

- **Recommended band thickness for pave bands**: 2.0mm minimum, with 2.2mm preferred.
- **Recommended band width for single-row pave**: 1.8mm to 2.2mm depending on pave diamond size.

For cathedral settings and other designs where the band must support significant structural elements above it, thickness should be at the higher end of these ranges.

#### Why Thin Bands Fail

A band that is too thin will eventually warp from the cumulative stress of daily wear -- gripping objects, bumping surfaces, removing the ring. A warped band causes the ring to lose its round shape, which affects fit and can stress the setting enough to compromise stone security. Furthermore, a thin band wears down more quickly, and there is simply less metal available for future resizing or repair.

Some designs -- particularly ultra-thin, dainty styles -- use band dimensions below our recommended minimums. These rings can be attractive, but buyers should understand that they are accepting a trade-off between aesthetics and longevity. A 1.2mm band in 18k gold will not withstand decades of daily wear without significant maintenance or eventual replacement.

### Surface Finishing

#### Polish Quality

The surface of a ring should exhibit the quality of finish appropriate to its intended style. For a high-polish (mirror) finish, the surface should be free of visible scratches, swirl marks, and haze when examined under magnification. The reflection should be crisp and undistorted.

Common polish defects include:

**Grinding marks**: Linear scratches left by abrasive wheels during the shaping process. These should be completely removed during progressive polishing stages. If they are visible in the final product, the polishing was insufficient.

**Orange peel**: A subtly bumpy texture resembling the surface of an orange, caused by over-polishing or polishing with too much heat. The surface appears shiny to the naked eye but reveals an irregular texture under magnification.

**Tool marks**: Impressions from setting tools, pliers, or other implements that were not polished out during finishing. These are most commonly found near the prongs, inside the basket, and on the inner surface of the band.

#### Finish Types

**Mirror (high polish)**: The standard for most engagement rings. The surface is polished to a highly reflective state. Mirror finish shows scratches most readily with wear, but scratches can be polished out.

**Satin (matte)**: A fine, uniform texture that produces a soft, non-reflective surface. Satin finishes are created using fine abrasives or brushing techniques. They hide minor scratches better than mirror finishes but will gradually develop a shine in high-wear areas over time.

**Brushed**: Similar to satin but with visible directional lines. Brushed finishes have a modern, understated quality. They can be re-brushed relatively easily to restore their original appearance.

**Hammered**: A textured surface created by striking the metal with a hammer or die. Hammered finishes are distinctive and hide wear well, but they are relatively uncommon in traditional engagement ring designs.

### Overall Symmetry

Step back from the individual components and assess the ring as a whole. Hold it at arm's length and view it from above, from the side, and from an angle.

- Is the diamond centred on the band?
- Do the two sides of the ring mirror each other?
- Is the band consistently round when viewed from above?
- Does the ring sit flat on a surface, or does it wobble?
- Is the transition from band to setting smooth and organic, or abrupt and awkward?

Symmetry errors in the overall ring are easier to spot than individual component defects, and they are a reliable general indicator of manufacturing quality. A ring that looks "right" from all angles was almost certainly made with care. A ring that looks slightly off -- even if you cannot immediately identify why -- likely has underlying quality issues worth investigating.

### What to Look For: An Inspection Checklist

Before finalising a purchase, systematically check each of the following:

**Prongs**
- [ ] All prongs are evenly spaced
- [ ] All prongs are the same height
- [ ] Prong tips are smooth, rounded, and polished
- [ ] No gaps between prongs and diamond
- [ ] Prong seats are properly cut (not too deep, not too shallow)
- [ ] Prong thickness is appropriate for the stone size

**Diamond Seating**
- [ ] Diamond sits level (table parallel to the band plane)
- [ ] Diamond does not rock or shift when gently probed
- [ ] Diamond is centred in the setting
- [ ] No visible gaps between stone and setting on any side

**Pave (if applicable)**
- [ ] All pave stones are at a consistent height
- [ ] Spacing between pave stones is uniform
- [ ] Pave surface feels smooth to the touch (no stones sitting high or low)
- [ ] Beads/prongs holding pave stones are well-formed and secure
- [ ] No visible tool marks between pave stones

**Casting and Structure**
- [ ] No surface pitting or porosity
- [ ] No visible sprue marks
- [ ] Basket/gallery is symmetrical
- [ ] All structural junctions are clean and solid
- [ ] No sharp edges anywhere on the ring

**Band**
- [ ] Band thickness meets minimums (1.6mm absolute minimum, 1.8-2.0mm preferred)
- [ ] Band is consistently round
- [ ] Ring sits flat on a surface without wobbling
- [ ] Inside of band is smooth and comfortable

**Surface Finishing**
- [ ] No grinding marks, tool marks, or polishing defects
- [ ] Finish is consistent across all surfaces
- [ ] Inside of band is finished to same standard as outside
- [ ] No discolouration or rough spots

**Overall**
- [ ] Ring is symmetrical from all viewing angles
- [ ] Diamond appears centred and properly proportioned relative to the band
- [ ] No component appears rushed, unfinished, or substandard
- [ ] Ring feels solid and well-made when held

If any items on this checklist give you pause, ask questions. A reputable seller will welcome your scrutiny and address your concerns directly. If a seller is dismissive of your inspection efforts or reluctant to provide magnification, consider that a significant warning sign.

# Section 3: Diamond Cut Quality — The Factor That Matters Most

### What "Cut" Actually Means

Of the 4Cs, Cut is the most misunderstood -- and paradoxically, it is the most important. Many buyers conflate "cut" with "shape." They are entirely different things.

**Shape** refers to the outline of the diamond when viewed from above: round, oval, cushion, emerald, pear, and so on. Shape is a matter of personal preference.

**Cut** refers to how well the diamond's facets have been angled, proportioned, and polished to interact with light. Cut is a matter of measurable quality. A well-cut diamond takes the light that enters through its top surface, bounces it internally off precisely angled pavilion facets, and returns it to the viewer's eye as brilliance, fire, and scintillation. A poorly cut diamond allows light to leak out through the bottom or sides, resulting in a stone that appears dull, dark, or lifeless despite potentially excellent colour and clarity grades.

#### The Three Components of Light Performance

**Brilliance** is the total amount of white light returned to the viewer's eye. When you look at a diamond and see bright flashes of white light, that is brilliance. It is the most immediately noticeable aspect of a diamond's beauty and the one that makes the biggest impression across a room. Brilliance is primarily a function of how well the crown and pavilion angles work together to create total internal reflection.

**Fire** is the dispersion of white light into its component spectral colours -- the flashes of red, orange, yellow, green, blue, and violet that appear as the diamond moves. Fire is created when light exits the diamond at an angle that causes it to separate into its wavelength components, much like a prism. Fire is most visible in controlled lighting conditions with directional light sources and is somewhat suppressed in diffuse overhead lighting.

**Scintillation** is the pattern of light and dark areas that shifts as the diamond, the viewer, or the light source moves. Good scintillation creates a lively, dynamic sparkle with a balanced pattern of bright and dark zones. Poor scintillation can manifest as large, dull areas (extinction) or a uniform, flat brightness that lacks life and contrast.

These three components work together. A diamond with excellent brilliance but no fire looks bright but monotone. A diamond with good fire but poor brilliance looks colourful but dim. A diamond with good brilliance and fire but poor scintillation can look "sleepy" or static. The best diamonds deliver all three in balance.

### Ideal Proportions

The proportions of a diamond's facets -- their angles and their sizes relative to each other -- determine how it handles light. While the full geometry of a round brilliant diamond involves 57 or 58 facets with complex interrelationships, several key measurements capture most of what matters.

#### Table Percentage

The table percentage is the width of the table facet (the large flat facet on top) expressed as a percentage of the diamond's total diameter. For round brilliant diamonds, the ideal range is generally **54% to 58%**. Tables in the 53% to 60% range can still produce excellent light performance, but outside this range, issues begin to appear.

A table that is too large (above approximately 62%) reduces the area available for the crown facets, which limits fire production. The diamond may be bright but will lack the spectral colour flashes that give diamonds their distinctive character. If you were to compare a 62% table diamond with a 56% table diamond in angled lighting, the difference in fire would be noticeable -- the larger-table stone would flash primarily white, while the smaller-table stone would throw distinct rainbow colours.

A table that is too small (below approximately 52%) produces excessive fire at the expense of brilliance. The diamond may display vivid spectral colours but appear dimmer overall, particularly in diffuse lighting.

#### Total Depth Percentage

Total depth percentage is the height of the diamond from table to culet, expressed as a percentage of the diameter. For round brilliants, the ideal range is approximately **59% to 62.5%**. This measurement is directly related to the crown and pavilion proportions.

A diamond that is too deep (above approximately 64%) is concentrating its weight in height rather than diameter. Looking at two diamonds side by side -- one with a 60% depth and one with a 66% depth, both weighing 1.00ct -- the deeper stone would appear noticeably smaller from above. The light that enters the deep stone tends to leak out through the pavilion rather than returning to the eye, creating a dark centre or a ring of darkness around the centre when viewed face-up.

A diamond that is too shallow (below approximately 57%) spreads its weight across a wider diameter, which can seem advantageous -- the stone faces up larger. But the shallow pavilion angles cause light to pass straight through the bottom of the stone rather than reflecting back to the viewer. If you were to place a shallow-cut diamond on a printed page, you might see the text through the stone, sometimes referred to as the "fish-eye" effect. Looking down at such a diamond, you would notice a distinct ring-like reflection of the girdle visible through the table, and the overall appearance would be watery and washed-out rather than brilliant.

#### Crown Angle

The crown angle is the angle between the crown facets and the table plane. The ideal range for round brilliants is approximately **34.0 to 35.0 degrees**. The crown angle primarily influences fire -- steeper crown angles produce more fire, while shallower crown angles produce less.

#### Pavilion Angle

The pavilion angle is the angle between the pavilion facets and the girdle plane. The ideal range is approximately **40.6 to 41.0 degrees**, and this is the single most critical proportion in determining a diamond's light performance. Pavilion angle has the greatest influence on brilliance.

A pavilion angle that is too steep (above 41.2 degrees) causes light to exit through the opposite side of the pavilion rather than reflecting back through the crown. This creates a visible pattern of darkness when the diamond is viewed face-up -- an effect commonly called "nail head," because the dark area in the centre of the stone resembles the head of a nail. If you imagined looking straight down into such a diamond, you would see a dark circle in the centre surrounded by brighter areas at the edges.

A pavilion angle that is too shallow (below 40.4 degrees) fails to create total internal reflection, allowing light to leak through the bottom. This produces the "fish-eye" effect described above and results in a diamond that appears glassy and transparent rather than brilliant.

#### The Relationship Between Proportions

Crown angle and pavilion angle do not operate independently -- they work as a complementary pair. A slightly steep pavilion angle can be compensated by a slightly shallow crown angle, and vice versa. This is why evaluating cut based on any single measurement is unreliable. The GIA and AGS cut grading systems assess proportions holistically, taking into account the interactions between multiple measurements.

### Cut Grade Systems

#### GIA Cut Grading (Round Brilliant Only)

The GIA grades cut on a five-point scale: **Excellent, Very Good, Good, Fair, and Poor**. This grade applies only to round brilliant diamonds -- GIA does not assign cut grades to fancy shapes.

The GIA system evaluates seven components: brightness, fire, scintillation, weight ratio, durability, polish, and symmetry. The overall cut grade is determined by the lowest-performing component, which means a diamond with excellent proportions but only Very Good polish will receive an overall grade of Very Good.

A GIA Excellent cut grade is a meaningful distinction. The grading criteria are based on extensive research including observation studies with trained and untrained observers. However, it is important to understand that "Excellent" represents a range, not a single point. Not all GIA Excellent diamonds are equally beautiful -- some are at the top of the range and some are at the bottom. This is why examining the specific proportions on the grading report, rather than relying solely on the overall grade, is valuable.

#### AGS Cut Grading

The American Gem Society uses a numerical scale from **0 (Ideal) to 10 (Poor)**. AGS 0, or AGS Ideal, is a stricter standard than GIA Excellent -- the acceptable proportion ranges are narrower, and the grading incorporates ray-tracing light performance analysis.

AGS grades cut for both round brilliants and certain fancy shapes, which is an advantage over GIA's system. An AGS Ideal round brilliant is, on average, a better-cut diamond than a GIA Excellent round brilliant, though the practical difference is often subtle and both represent very high quality.

#### How Labs Differ

The differences between GIA and AGS cut grading can be summarised as follows: GIA Excellent encompasses a wider range of proportions and does not include direct light performance measurement. AGS Ideal is a tighter standard that includes light performance analysis. A diamond that is AGS Ideal will almost certainly be GIA Excellent, but a diamond that is GIA Excellent may or may not qualify as AGS Ideal.

Neither system is "wrong" -- they simply draw their boundaries in slightly different places. For consumers seeking the best possible cut quality and willing to be selective, AGS Ideal or the top tier of GIA Excellent (identifiable by examining specific proportions) represents the highest standard readily available.

### The Round Brilliant Cut

The round brilliant is the most optically efficient diamond shape and the most thoroughly studied. Its 57 or 58 facets (58 if a culet facet is present) have been mathematically optimised over more than a century of research.

#### The Tolkowsky Ideal

In 1919, Marcel Tolkowsky published his mathematical analysis of the ideal proportions for a round brilliant diamond. His work established proportions that became the basis for the modern ideal cut: a table of 53%, crown angle of 34.5 degrees, and pavilion angle of 40.75 degrees.

Modern research, including the GIA's extensive modelling work, has refined Tolkowsky's proportions and demonstrated that excellent light performance can be achieved across a range of proportion combinations rather than a single set of "ideal" numbers. Nevertheless, Tolkowsky's work remains foundational, and many premium cut diamonds are produced with proportions close to his original specifications.

#### Modern Ideal Parameters

Based on current research and industry consensus, the parameters most consistently associated with top-tier light performance in round brilliant diamonds are:

- Table: 54-58%
- Depth: 59.5-62.2%
- Crown angle: 34.0-35.0 degrees
- Pavilion angle: 40.6-40.9 degrees
- Lower half facet length: 75-80%
- Star facet length: 45-55%
- Girdle: Thin to slightly thick (not extremely thin or very thick)

Diamonds falling within these ranges with good symmetry and polish will, in the vast majority of cases, deliver excellent light performance.

#### Hearts and Arrows

Hearts and Arrows (H&A) is an optical phenomenon visible in extremely well-cut round brilliant diamonds. When viewed through a specialised scope, the diamond displays eight symmetrical arrows from above and eight symmetrical hearts from below. The pattern results from precise optical symmetry -- each facet aligned exactly with its counterpart on the opposite side of the diamond.

A true H&A pattern confirms excellent optical symmetry but does not guarantee ideal proportions. A diamond can display a perfect H&A pattern while having proportions outside the ideal range, and conversely, a diamond with ideal proportions may have slightly imperfect symmetry that prevents a clean H&A pattern. Most diamonds sold as H&A do have excellent proportions, but the designation should be verified independently.

### Fancy Shapes

Fancy shape diamonds -- anything other than round -- are cut to different geometric patterns and have different proportion guidelines. Critically, the GIA does not assign cut grades to fancy shapes, which means the consumer must evaluate cut quality by examining proportions, symmetry, and visual appearance directly.

#### Oval

The oval brilliant combines the brilliance of a round with an elongated shape. Standard length-to-width (L:W) ratios range from 1.30:1 to 1.50:1, with 1.35:1 to 1.45:1 being the most popular. Below 1.30:1 the stone looks nearly round; above 1.55:1, it appears narrow.

The primary concern with ovals is the **bow-tie effect**: a dark, bow-tie-shaped shadow across the centre when viewed face-up. Some degree of bow-tie is inherent to the shape's geometry. A faint bow-tie is acceptable; a strong, dark one creates a conspicuous stripe across the middle -- the diamond appears bright at its ends but has a distinct dark band through the centre, as though you were looking through a pair of sunglasses at the stone's midsection. No grading report quantifies bow-tie severity; this must be evaluated visually.

#### Cushion

Cushion cuts combine a square or rectangular outline with rounded corners. Two types exist: **cushion brilliant** (broader facets producing bold flashes) and **cushion modified brilliant** (an extra facet row creating a dispersed "crushed ice" sparkle). These look markedly different in person, so buyers should confirm which style they are purchasing. L:W ratios range from 1.00:1 (square) to 1.30:1 (rectangular).

#### Emerald and Asscher

The emerald cut is a step cut with rectangular facets in parallel rows, creating a "hall of mirrors" effect -- long, geometric flashes rather than fragmented sparkle. Because step cuts do not break up light as aggressively, they reveal colour and inclusions more transparently. Buyers choosing emerald cuts should consider higher colour and clarity grades. Ideal L:W ratios are 1.30:1 to 1.50:1. The asscher is essentially a square step cut with a higher crown, producing slightly more brilliance while maintaining the step-cut aesthetic.

#### Pear and Marquise

The pear (teardrop) combines a rounded end with a pointed end. L:W ratios of 1.50:1 to 1.65:1 are most balanced. Pears are susceptible to the bow-tie effect, and the point must be sharp, symmetrical, and protected with a V-prong when set.

The marquise has two pointed ends and the largest face-up area per carat of any standard shape. L:W ratios of 1.85:1 to 2.10:1 are typical. Both points must align precisely on the longitudinal axis and be protected with V-prongs. Bow-tie is a significant concern.

#### Princess and Radiant

The princess cut is a square brilliant with pointed corners offering excellent sparkle. All four corners are vulnerable to chipping and must be protected with V-prongs -- this is non-negotiable. The radiant combines an emerald-cut outline (with trimmed corners) and brilliant-style faceting, producing more sparkle than an emerald cut with less chipping risk than a princess.

### How Cut Affects Face-Up Size

This is one of the most practically important aspects of cut quality, and it is frequently overlooked.

A diamond's carat weight tells you its mass, not its visible size. Two 1.00ct round diamonds can differ meaningfully in how large they appear when viewed from above, depending on how their weight is distributed.

Consider two round diamonds, both certified as 1.00ct:

**Diamond A** is well-proportioned with a depth of 61.5% and a diameter of approximately 6.4mm. This is the expected size for a 1.00ct round.

**Diamond B** is deeply cut with a depth of 65.0% and a diameter of only 6.1mm. The extra weight is hidden in the pavilion depth, invisible from above.

Diamond A presents a face-up area of approximately 32.2 square millimetres. Diamond B presents approximately 29.2 square millimetres -- nearly 10% less visible area for the same carat weight. Diamond B effectively looks like a well-cut 0.85ct stone despite weighing a full carat. You are paying for weight that you cannot see.

The reverse is also true: a well-cut 0.90ct diamond with a diameter of 6.2mm will face up nearly as large as a poorly cut 1.10ct diamond, but at significantly lower cost. This is why understanding spread (the face-up dimensions in millimetres) is essential to getting genuine value.

Here are approximate ideal diameters by carat weight for round brilliant diamonds, which you can use to evaluate whether a particular diamond is carrying its weight well:

- 0.50ct: 5.1-5.2mm
- 0.70ct: 5.7-5.8mm
- 0.90ct: 6.1-6.3mm
- 1.00ct: 6.3-6.5mm
- 1.20ct: 6.8-6.9mm
- 1.50ct: 7.3-7.5mm
- 2.00ct: 8.1-8.2mm

If the diameter of a diamond you are considering falls significantly below these ranges for its weight, you are paying for depth that adds nothing to the visual experience.

### Cut Quality vs Price

Diamonds with Excellent or Ideal cut grades command a premium, and this premium is worth paying. The visual difference between an Excellent-cut and a Good-cut diamond is not subtle. The Excellent stone appears more brilliant, more lively, and more "diamondlike." The Good-cut stone appears dimmer and less engaging.

Cut grade also affects how the diamond handles the other 3Cs. A well-cut diamond minimises the visibility of body colour because intense light return overpowers subtle warmth. It can make certain inclusions less visible because the fragmented light pattern obscures them. And it appears larger for its weight because proportions are optimised for face-up size. A poorly cut diamond amplifies all of these weaknesses.

The price premium for Excellent over Very Good is typically 10-15%; over Good, 20-30%. This is the single best investment in the diamond selection process. If budget is constrained, choose a slightly lower colour or clarity to afford better cut rather than compromising on cut quality.

### How to Evaluate Cut

#### Using the Certificate

The grading report provides the raw data you need. For round brilliants, check:

1. **Overall cut grade**: GIA Excellent or AGS Ideal is the starting point.
2. **Polish**: Should be Excellent or Very Good. Anything lower indicates surface imperfections that affect light performance.
3. **Symmetry**: Should be Excellent or Very Good. Lower symmetry grades indicate facet misalignment.
4. **Table percentage, depth percentage, crown angle, pavilion angle**: Compare these to the ideal ranges provided earlier in this section.

For fancy shapes without a cut grade, the proportions and symmetry data on the report become your primary tools. Research the ideal proportion ranges for your specific shape and compare.

#### ASET and IdealScope Images

For buyers who want to go beyond the certificate, two specialised imaging tools provide direct visualisation of light performance.

**ASET (Angular Spectrum Evaluation Tool)** images use colour coding to show where a diamond draws its light from. Red indicates light from steep angles (directly above), green indicates light from lower angles (the environment), and blue indicates light blockage (which creates desirable contrast). A well-cut diamond shows predominantly red and green with strategically placed blue. A poorly cut diamond shows excessive green (light leakage through the pavilion).

**IdealScope** images are simpler: red where the diamond returns light, white where light leaks through. If you were comparing two diamonds, the well-cut stone would present as a mostly solid red pattern with small, symmetrical white zones. The poorly cut stone would show large, irregular white patches -- in the centre (a "nail head" from a steep pavilion) or in a ring around the centre (a "fish-eye" from a shallow pavilion).

These images are not routinely included on grading reports, but many vendors provide them on request, particularly online vendors who specialise in high-quality diamonds.

### Common Misconceptions

#### "Triple Excellent" Means the Best

"Triple Excellent" (3EX) refers to a GIA-graded diamond receiving Excellent for cut, polish, and symmetry. While this is a good standard, GIA Excellent represents a range -- two Triple Excellent diamonds can look meaningfully different. A diamond with Excellent cut, Excellent polish, and Very Good symmetry may actually look better than some 3EX stones if its proportions fall in a more optically optimal zone.

Triple Excellent is a useful minimum standard, but it should be the beginning of your evaluation, not the end. Examine the specific proportions and request light performance images where possible.

#### Hearts and Arrows Means Perfect Cut

The H&A pattern confirms optical symmetry but does not guarantee ideal proportions. The label is not standardised or regulated -- there is no industry-wide definition of how perfect the pattern must be to qualify. When a diamond is marketed as H&A, ask to see the scope images and judge the pattern's precision for yourself.

#### Higher Carat Weight Always Looks Bigger

Carat weight is not the same as visual size. A well-cut diamond of lower carat weight can look as large as a poorly cut diamond of higher carat weight. Always check the millimetre dimensions against the ideal ranges provided earlier. Do not pay a premium for weight buried in excessive depth.

#### Lab Reports Tell You Everything

A grading report tells you measurable characteristics but not how the diamond looks in real life. Two diamonds with identical report data can look different due to the precise distribution of inclusions, fluorescence effects, and minor proportion variations within the reported range. Use the report as a screening tool, then evaluate your shortlisted diamonds through images, video, and ideally in-person viewing.

# Section 4: Lab-Grown vs Natural Diamonds — A Genuine Comparison

## Introduction

Few topics in the jewellery industry generate as much debate — or as much misinformation — as the choice between lab-grown and natural diamonds. Depending on whom you ask, lab-grown diamonds are either the future of ethical jewellery or a worthless imitation. Natural diamonds are either geological miracles or environmentally destructive relics of an exploitative industry.

The truth, as is usually the case, is considerably more nuanced.

At House of Diamonds, we sell both natural and lab-grown diamonds. We have done so for over three decades. That means we have no financial incentive to steer you in one direction over another — our margins are healthy on both, and our reputation depends on customers who feel they were given honest advice. What follows is our genuine attempt to lay out the facts, the uncertainties, and the trade-offs so that you can make a decision that reflects your own values and circumstances.

This is not a simple question with a simple answer. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

---

## What Lab-Grown Diamonds Actually Are

A lab-grown diamond is a diamond. Full stop. It is not a simulant, not an imitation, and not a synthetic in the way most people understand that word. It is crystallised carbon with the same atomic structure, the same physical properties, and the same optical behaviour as a diamond formed beneath the earth's surface. The Federal Trade Commission in the United States, the International Gemological Institute, and every major grading laboratory in the world recognise lab-grown diamonds as real diamonds.

This distinction matters because lab-grown diamonds are frequently confused with diamond simulants — materials that look like diamonds but have entirely different chemical compositions. Cubic zirconia (CZ) is zirconium dioxide. Moissanite is silicon carbide. Neither is carbon, neither has the hardness of diamond, and neither behaves like diamond under magnification or spectroscopic analysis. A lab-grown diamond, by contrast, is indistinguishable from a natural diamond by any visual or physical test available to a jeweller using standard equipment.

### How They Are Made

There are two primary methods for growing diamonds in a laboratory setting.

**High Pressure High Temperature (HPHT)** replicates the conditions under which natural diamonds form in the earth's mantle — roughly 1,500 degrees Celsius and pressures exceeding 1.5 million pounds per square inch. A small diamond seed crystal is placed in a chamber with a carbon source (typically graphite) and a metallic catalyst. Under extreme heat and pressure, the carbon dissolves and crystallises around the seed, growing a rough diamond crystal over a period of days to weeks depending on the target size.

HPHT was the first commercially viable method for producing gem-quality diamonds. The resulting crystals tend to grow in a cuboctahedral shape and may exhibit a slight yellowish or brownish tint due to nitrogen incorporation, though modern HPHT processes can produce colourless stones consistently. HPHT diamonds sometimes display a metallic flux inclusion — a tiny remnant of the catalyst material — which is one of the ways gemological laboratories can identify their origin.

**Chemical Vapour Deposition (CVD)** takes a different approach. A thin diamond seed plate is placed in a vacuum chamber, which is then filled with a carbon-rich gas — typically methane mixed with hydrogen. The gas is ionised using microwave energy, breaking the molecular bonds and releasing individual carbon atoms, which settle onto the seed plate and crystallise layer by layer. The process occurs at lower pressures than HPHT but still requires temperatures around 800 to 1,200 degrees Celsius.

CVD diamonds tend to grow in a flat, tabular shape and are generally considered to produce more consistently colourless results. The process is also more scalable, which is one reason CVD has become the dominant production method for gem-quality lab-grown diamonds in recent years. CVD diamonds may exhibit a faint brownish or greyish undertone in their rough state, which is often corrected through post-growth HPHT treatment — a fact that is disclosed on laboratory grading reports.

Both methods produce genuine diamonds. The choice of growth method affects the economics and certain trace characteristics of the stone but has no bearing on whether the end product is a "real" diamond. It is.

### Certification and Grading

Lab-grown diamonds are graded using the same 4Cs framework (cut, colour, clarity, carat weight) as natural diamonds. The major grading laboratories — GIA (Gemological Institute of America) and IGI (International Gemological Institute) — both issue reports for lab-grown diamonds, clearly identified as such.

GIA began issuing full grading reports for lab-grown diamonds in 2020, having previously offered only a more limited identification report. IGI has graded lab-grown diamonds with full reports for considerably longer and currently processes the majority of lab-grown diamond certifications worldwide. Both laboratories laser-inscribe the girdle of the diamond with a report number and the designation "Laboratory Grown" or "LG" to ensure permanent identification.

It is worth noting that grading, whether for natural or lab-grown diamonds, involves human judgement and is not perfectly consistent between laboratories or even between different graders at the same laboratory. This is true for natural diamonds as well. We discuss the implications of grading variability in detail in Section 2 of this guide.

## Chemical, Optical, and Physical Properties

This is the section that settles the most common misconception. Lab-grown and natural diamonds are not merely similar — they are identical in every measurable physical property.

| Property | Natural Diamond | Lab-Grown Diamond |
|---|---|---|
| Chemical composition | Pure carbon (C) | Pure carbon (C) |
| Crystal structure | Cubic (isometric) | Cubic (isometric) |
| Hardness (Mohs scale) | 10 | 10 |
| Refractive index | 2.417 | 2.417 |
| Dispersion | 0.044 | 0.044 |
| Specific gravity | 3.52 | 3.52 |
| Thermal conductivity | ~2,200 W/mK | ~2,200 W/mK |

A standard diamond tester — the handheld device jewellers use to distinguish diamonds from simulants — will return a positive result for both natural and lab-grown diamonds, because it measures thermal conductivity, which is identical in both.

Even trained gemologists cannot distinguish a lab-grown diamond from a natural one using a standard loupe or microscope. Identification requires specialised spectroscopic equipment — specifically, photoluminescence spectroscopy or infrared absorption spectroscopy — which can detect subtle differences in trace element distribution and crystal growth patterns. These differences exist because the growth environment (a laboratory chamber versus the earth's mantle over billions of years) leaves distinct signatures at the atomic level, but they have no effect on the diamond's appearance, durability, or performance.

In practical terms, a lab-grown diamond will sparkle the same way, wear the same way, and last the same way as a natural diamond. Your grandmother will not be able to tell the difference. Neither will your jeweller, unless they have access to laboratory-grade detection equipment costing tens of thousands of dollars.

## Cost Comparison

This is where the conversation gets interesting — and where the landscape has shifted dramatically in recent years.

Lab-grown diamonds have always been less expensive than natural diamonds, but the gap has widened considerably since 2020. Several factors have driven this: increased production capacity (particularly from facilities in India and China), improved CVD technology allowing faster and more consistent growth, and market competition among a growing number of producers.

The following price ranges are approximate and reflect the Australian market as of mid-2025. They assume round brilliant cut, G-H colour, VS1-VS2 clarity, and excellent or ideal cut grade. Prices vary significantly based on exact specifications, certification laboratory, and retailer.

### Approximate Retail Prices (AUD)

| Carat Weight | Natural Diamond | Lab-Grown Diamond | Approximate Saving |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.50 ct | $2,500 – $4,500 | $500 – $1,200 | 65–80% |
| 1.00 ct | $8,000 – $15,000 | $1,500 – $3,500 | 75–85% |

These figures represent a significant shift from even five years ago. In 2020, a 1-carat lab-grown diamond of comparable quality might have retailed for $5,000 to $8,000 AUD — still a substantial saving over natural, but considerably more than today's prices. The trajectory has been consistently downward for lab-grown diamonds, while natural diamond prices have remained relatively stable or increased modestly for higher-quality stones.

What this means in practice is that a buyer who might have budgeted $10,000 for a 1-carat natural diamond engagement ring could instead purchase a 2-carat lab-grown diamond ring of equivalent quality for a similar outlay — or a 1-carat lab-grown diamond ring and have several thousand dollars remaining for the wedding, a honeymoon, or a house deposit.

The cost advantage is real and substantial. It is the single most compelling reason buyers choose lab-grown diamonds, and there is nothing wrong with that. A diamond's beauty is not determined by its price tag.

### Why the Price Difference Exists

The cost difference comes down to supply economics. Natural diamonds are finite — there is a fixed quantity in the earth's crust, extraction is expensive and slow, and the supply chain involves mining, sorting, cutting, and distribution across multiple continents. Lab-grown diamonds can be produced in virtually unlimited quantities with relatively predictable costs. As production technology improves and scales, unit costs continue to fall.

This is also why we need to have an honest conversation about resale value.

## Resale Value — An Honest Discussion

This is the section most jewellers would prefer to skip. We think it is one of the most important.

### Natural Diamonds and Resale

Let us begin with an uncomfortable truth about natural diamonds: they are not a good financial investment either.

The diamond industry has spent decades cultivating the perception that diamonds hold their value. In reality, if you purchase a natural diamond engagement ring from a retail jeweller and attempt to sell it the following week, you will typically recover 30 to 50 percent of what you paid. This is not because the diamond has depreciated — it is because the retail price included the jeweller's margin, overhead, marketing costs, and the multiple markups applied at each stage of the supply chain from mine to retail counter.

The wholesale market for pre-owned natural diamonds is competitive, and buyers — whether pawnbrokers, second-hand jewellers, or diamond dealers — will offer wholesale prices at best. A $15,000 engagement ring might fetch $5,000 to $8,000 on the second-hand market if the diamond is well-certified and the buyer is motivated.

Exceptional natural diamonds — those of significant size (3 carats and above) with top colour and clarity grades — can hold value more effectively and have, over very long time horizons, appreciated. But the typical engagement ring diamond (0.5 to 1.5 carats, G-I colour, VS-SI clarity) is not in this category. It is a consumer product, not an investment asset.

### Lab-Grown Diamonds and Resale

Lab-grown diamonds have significantly lower resale value than natural diamonds, and this gap has widened as production costs have fallen. A lab-grown diamond purchased today for $3,000 may have a resale value of $300 to $600 — or potentially less, given that an equivalent new stone could be purchased for a similar or lower price by the time you attempt to sell.

This is a genuine disadvantage, and we would be dishonest to minimise it. If retaining financial value in the stone is important to you, a natural diamond is the better choice.

### Putting Resale in Perspective

However, we would encourage you to consider the role of resale value in your decision critically.

Most engagement rings are never resold. They are worn for a lifetime, passed down to children, or reset into new settings. The scenarios in which resale value matters — divorce, financial hardship, or simply changing taste — are real but statistically uncommon motivations at the point of purchase.

Consider the analogy of a new car. A $60,000 car loses 20 to 30 percent of its value the moment you drive it off the lot. Within five years, it may be worth half what you paid. Yet we do not generally advise people against buying new cars on the grounds that they are poor investments. We accept that a car provides utility, pleasure, and transportation, and that the cost is the price of those benefits.

An engagement ring provides a different kind of value — emotional, symbolic, aesthetic — but the principle is similar. If you spend $10,000 on a natural diamond ring and it is worth $5,000 on resale, you have "lost" $5,000. If you spend $3,000 on a lab-grown diamond ring of equivalent visual impact and it is worth $500 on resale, you have "lost" $2,500. In absolute terms, the lab-grown buyer is financially ahead, despite the lower resale percentage.

Neither diamond is an investment. Both are purchases. The question is how much you want to spend on the purchase, not how much you expect to recover.

## Environmental and Ethical Considerations

This is perhaps the most contentious area of the lab-grown versus natural debate, and it is where the most misleading claims are made — by both sides.

### The Case Against Diamond Mining

The environmental impact of diamond mining is real and well-documented. Open-pit diamond mines can displace enormous quantities of earth — some of the largest mines in the world, such as the Mir mine in Russia, are visible from space. Mining operations consume significant energy and water, generate waste rock and tailings, and can cause habitat destruction, soil erosion, and water contamination.

The social impact of diamond mining has an equally complex history. The term "blood diamonds" or "conflict diamonds" refers to stones mined in war zones and sold to finance armed conflict, primarily in West and Central Africa during the 1990s and early 2000s. The Kimberley Process Certification Scheme was established in 2003 to prevent the trade in conflict diamonds, and it has had measurable success — the proportion of conflict diamonds in the global supply is estimated to have fallen from approximately 15 percent in the late 1990s to less than 1 percent today.

However, the Kimberley Process has significant limitations. It defines "conflict diamonds" narrowly — only stones used to finance rebel movements against recognised governments. It does not address diamonds mined under exploitative labour conditions, environmental degradation, or by state actors engaged in human rights abuses. Critics, including the organisation Global Witness (which helped establish the Kimberley Process and later withdrew from it), argue that the scheme provides a veneer of legitimacy without addressing the broader ethical concerns of diamond mining.

### The Case for Nuance on Lab-Grown

Lab-grown diamonds are frequently marketed as "eco-friendly," "sustainable," or "conflict-free." These claims deserve scrutiny.

Growing diamonds in a laboratory requires substantial energy. A CVD reactor producing a 1-carat rough diamond may consume 250 to 750 kilowatt-hours of electricity, depending on the efficiency of the equipment, the growth time, and the target quality. HPHT production can require even more energy due to the extreme pressures involved.

The environmental footprint of that energy depends entirely on the source. A lab-grown diamond produced using renewable energy in a facility powered by hydroelectric or solar generation has a genuinely lower carbon footprint than a mined diamond. A lab-grown diamond produced in a facility powered by coal-fired electricity — as is the case for a significant proportion of production in India and China — may have a carbon footprint comparable to or even exceeding that of a responsibly mined natural diamond.

The challenge for consumers is that supply chain transparency in the lab-grown diamond industry is often limited. Many retailers cannot tell you where their lab-grown diamonds were grown, what energy source was used, or what the actual carbon footprint of the stone is. Claims of "carbon neutral" or "sustainable" production should be supported by third-party verification — and frequently they are not.

Some producers are making genuine efforts toward sustainability. Certain facilities in the United States and Europe operate on renewable energy and provide verified carbon footprint data. The Diamond Foundry, for example, has published third-party audited sustainability reports. But these represent a minority of global production.

### The Honest Position

The honest position is this: lab-grown diamonds avoid the specific harms associated with mining — land disruption, water use, the residual risk of conflict sourcing, and exploitative labour practices. On these dimensions, lab-grown diamonds have a clear ethical advantage.

However, lab-grown diamonds are not automatically "green" or "sustainable." Their environmental footprint depends on production methods and energy sources, which vary widely and are often opaque.

Natural diamonds, for their part, are not automatically "dirty" or "unethical." Responsible mining operations — particularly those in countries like Canada, Australia (prior to the closure of the Argyle mine), and Botswana — operate under stringent environmental regulations, provide significant employment and economic benefit to local communities, and produce stones that can be traced through the supply chain.

There is no simple good-versus-bad narrative here. Both options have trade-offs. Your choice should reflect which trade-offs matter most to you.

## When to Choose a Lab-Grown Diamond

A lab-grown diamond may be the right choice for you if:

**Budget is a primary concern.** If you have a fixed budget and want to maximise the size or quality of the diamond, lab-grown offers dramatically more value per dollar. A couple spending $5,000 can have a stunning 1.5-carat lab-grown diamond ring that would cost $20,000 or more as a natural diamond.

**You want a larger stone.** Some buyers simply want visual impact — a diamond that fills the setting and catches the light. Lab-grown makes carat weights of 2, 3, or even 4 carats accessible to buyers who would never consider those sizes in natural diamonds due to cost.

**Origin ethics are important to you.** If you are uncomfortable with any association with the mining industry, regardless of the nuances discussed above, a lab-grown diamond removes that concern entirely. There is no mine, no displacement, and no question about labour practices in the extraction process.

**You view the ring as a symbol rather than an asset.** If you see the engagement ring primarily as a meaningful gesture and a beautiful object, and you have no expectation of resale or financial return, the cost advantage of lab-grown is compelling.

**You prefer to allocate funds elsewhere.** Many couples today prefer to spend less on the ring and more on a home deposit, wedding, travel, or other shared priorities. Lab-grown diamonds make this possible without compromising on the visual result.

---

## When to Choose a Natural Diamond

A natural diamond may be the right choice for you if:

**Geological rarity has meaning for you.** A natural diamond formed one to three billion years ago, roughly 150 to 200 kilometres beneath the earth's surface, and was brought to the surface by volcanic eruption. If the idea that your diamond is older than complex life on earth and is genuinely unique carries emotional weight, that is a valid reason to choose natural. No laboratory process can replicate geological time.

**You value long-term financial retention.** While we have emphasised that diamonds are not investments, it remains true that natural diamonds retain more of their value over time than lab-grown diamonds. If the possibility of resale matters to you — whether for estate planning, insurance considerations, or personal preference — natural diamonds are the more conservative choice.

**Heirloom significance matters.** If the intention is to pass the ring down through generations, some buyers feel that a natural diamond carries a different symbolic weight. This is a personal and cultural consideration, and there is no objectively correct answer. A lab-grown diamond will last just as long — it is equally durable — but the narrative around it differs.

**You are purchasing a significant or rare stone.** For diamonds above 2 or 3 carats with exceptional colour and clarity, natural diamonds occupy a genuinely rare category. Collectors, connoisseurs, and buyers who appreciate scarcity for its own sake may find that the natural stone carries a significance that a lab-grown equivalent does not.

**Your partner has a preference.** This is perhaps the most practical consideration. If your partner has expressed a clear preference for a natural diamond, that preference matters more than any cost-benefit analysis. The ring is for them, and their feelings about it are the most important variable.

## What Some Jewellers Will Not Tell You

We believe in transparency, even when it is uncomfortable for our industry. Here are some things you may not hear from every jeweller.

**Financial incentives vary.** Some jewellers make higher margins on lab-grown diamonds than on natural diamonds, despite the lower retail price. Others make higher margins on natural. A jeweller's recommendation may be influenced by which product is more profitable for them. Ask directly what the margin structure looks like, or at least be aware that the recommendation you receive may not be purely objective.

**"Our lab-grown diamonds are special" is usually marketing.** Lab-grown diamonds from different retailers are largely sourced from the same handful of producers. Unless a jeweller owns or operates their own growing facility, they are purchasing from the same supply chain as everyone else. Claims of proprietary technology, exclusive sourcing, or superior lab-grown quality should be viewed sceptically unless supported by specific, verifiable evidence.

**Some jewellers refuse to sell lab-grown diamonds.** This is their prerogative, but the stated reasons — "they are not real diamonds," "they have no value," "we believe in natural diamonds" — often reflect a business decision rather than a gemological one. Lab-grown diamonds are real diamonds. They do have value. The decision not to sell them is commercial, not scientific.

**Conversely, some jewellers only sell lab-grown and overstate the ethical case.** Claiming that all natural diamonds are "blood diamonds" or that lab-grown diamonds are universally "eco-friendly" is equally misleading. Be cautious of any jeweller who presents a one-sided narrative, regardless of which side they favour.

**Certification laboratory choice matters.** Some lab-grown diamonds are certified by lesser-known laboratories whose grading standards may be more lenient than GIA or IGI. A "G colour, VS2 clarity" from an unfamiliar laboratory may not be equivalent to the same grade from GIA. Always ask which laboratory certified the stone, and be cautious if the jeweller is evasive about this detail.

## Making Your Decision — A Framework

Rather than telling you what to choose, we suggest working through these questions:

1. **What is your total budget for the engagement ring?** Understand what is possible in both natural and lab-grown at that budget. You may be surprised in both directions.

2. **How important is stone size to you or your partner?** If a larger diamond is a priority, lab-grown opens up options that may not be financially accessible in natural.

3. **How do you feel about resale value?** Be honest with yourself. If the idea that the stone might be worth very little in ten years bothers you, that is a legitimate concern. If you genuinely do not care about resale because you never intend to sell, that is equally legitimate.

4. **What are your ethical priorities?** Are you primarily concerned about mining practices, carbon footprint, labour conditions, or all of the above? Your answer may point in different directions, and that is acceptable.

5. **Does the origin story matter to you or your partner?** Some people are moved by the idea of a stone forged by geological forces over billions of years. Others are moved by the idea of a stone created by human ingenuity. Neither response is wrong.

6. **What does your partner actually want?** If you know, honour that preference. If you are unsure, this is a conversation worth having — even if it means sacrificing the surprise of the proposal. A ring your partner truly wants is worth more than a surprise they would not have chosen for themselves.

The right diamond is the one that aligns with your values, your budget, and your partner's preferences. It is not the one that generates the highest margin for the jeweller, wins the most arguments online, or conforms to someone else's idea of what an engagement ring should be.

It is yours. Make the choice that feels right for you.

# Section 5: Questions Every Buyer Should Ask Their Jeweller

## Introduction

Walking into a jewellery store — or opening an online chat with a jeweller — can be an intimidating experience. The terminology is unfamiliar, the prices are significant, and the emotional stakes are high. Most buyers have never purchased a diamond before and are relying heavily on the jeweller's expertise and honesty.

The best jewellers welcome questions. They understand that an informed buyer is a confident buyer, and that confidence translates into satisfaction, referrals, and long-term relationships. A jeweller who is uncomfortable with detailed questions is a jeweller you should be cautious about.

What follows is a comprehensive list of questions organised by category. For each question, we explain why it matters, what a good answer sounds like, and what evasive or inadequate responses might indicate. These questions apply whether you are buying from a bricks-and-mortar store, an online retailer, or a custom jeweller — and whether you are considering natural or lab-grown diamonds.

You do not need to ask every question on this list. But knowing which questions to ask — and how to evaluate the answers — puts you in a fundamentally stronger position as a buyer.

---

## Category 1: Questions About the Diamond

### "Can I see the GIA or IGI grading report for this specific diamond?"

**Why it matters:** A grading report from a reputable laboratory is the closest thing to an objective assessment of a diamond's quality. It confirms the 4Cs (cut, colour, clarity, carat weight), provides precise measurements, and includes a diagram of inclusions. Without a grading report, you are relying entirely on the jeweller's verbal claims, which cannot be independently verified.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller immediately provides a physical or digital copy of the grading report, explains the key details, and offers to walk you through it. The report should be from GIA, IGI, or another internationally recognised laboratory. The report number should match the laser inscription on the diamond's girdle, which the jeweller should be able to show you under magnification.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** Responses like "we do our own grading in-house," "a certificate is not really necessary for a stone this size," or "we use [obscure laboratory name] because they are more affordable" are warning signs. In-house grading has no independent verification. Unfamiliar laboratories may apply more generous grading standards, meaning the colour and clarity grades on their reports may not correspond to what GIA or IGI would assign. This is not a minor technicality — a one-grade difference in colour or clarity can represent a significant difference in value.

### "What is the cut grade, and can you explain the proportions?"

**Why it matters:** Cut is the single most important factor in a diamond's visual beauty. A well-cut diamond returns light to the eye with maximum brilliance and fire. A poorly cut diamond — even one with excellent colour and clarity — will look dull and lifeless. The cut grade on a grading report (Excellent, Very Good, Good, Fair, Poor) is a useful starting point, but the specific proportions — table percentage, depth percentage, crown angle, pavilion angle — provide more detailed information about how the diamond will perform optically.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller can explain the cut grade in context and discuss the specific proportions. For a round brilliant, they should be able to tell you the table percentage (ideally 54-58%), depth percentage (ideally 60-62.5%), crown angle (ideally 34-35 degrees), and pavilion angle (ideally 40.6-41 degrees). They should also be able to explain how these numbers interact — for example, that a slightly steeper crown angle can compensate for a slightly shallower pavilion angle.

For fancy shapes (oval, cushion, pear, emerald, etc.), GIA does not assign an overall cut grade, so the jeweller should be able to discuss proportions, symmetry, and — for shapes prone to it — the presence or absence of a bow-tie effect (a dark shadow across the centre of the stone visible in ovals, pears, and marquises). The jeweller should be willing to show you the stone in different lighting conditions so you can assess its performance yourself.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller cannot discuss proportions beyond "it is a very well-cut stone" or deflects the question toward colour or clarity, they may not have sufficient technical knowledge or may be attempting to sell a diamond whose cut does not warrant scrutiny. Cut is where jewellers can most easily obscure value — a stone that appears to be a bargain based on carat weight, colour, and clarity may be poorly cut, which is why the price is lower.

### "Is this diamond eye-clean?"

**Why it matters:** "Eye-clean" means that no inclusions are visible to the unaided eye when the diamond is viewed face-up at a normal viewing distance (approximately 20 to 30 centimetres). This is a practical standard that many buyers find more useful than the clarity grade alone. A VS2 diamond is almost always eye-clean. An SI1 diamond is usually eye-clean but not always. An SI2 diamond may or may not be eye-clean depending on the size, type, and location of the inclusions.

Buying an eye-clean SI1 rather than a VS1 can save you 15 to 25 percent with no visible difference in the mounted ring. This is one of the most effective ways to optimise your budget.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller will show you the diamond loose (unmounted) and invite you to examine it with your unaided eye. They should be able to point out where the inclusions are located on the grading report's plot diagram and explain whether they are likely to be visible once the stone is set. An honest jeweller will tell you if a stone is borderline eye-clean and may recommend a slightly higher clarity grade if the inclusions are in a prominent position (such as directly under the table facet).

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller only shows you the diamond under magnification, emphasises the clarity grade without discussing visibility, or says "all our diamonds are eye-clean" without demonstrating it, they may be avoiding a direct comparison. Some jewellers will also show the diamond in very bright, direct lighting (which can mask inclusions) rather than in normal ambient conditions. Ask to see the stone in natural daylight or standard indoor lighting.

### "What are the actual millimetre dimensions of the diamond?"

**Why it matters:** Carat weight measures mass, not size. Two diamonds of identical carat weight can have noticeably different face-up dimensions depending on how they are cut. A deeply cut 1-carat diamond might measure 6.1 mm across the top, while a well-proportioned 1-carat diamond should measure approximately 6.4 to 6.5 mm. The deep diamond carries its weight in the pavilion (the bottom half), which is hidden by the setting — meaning you are paying for carat weight you cannot see.

This effect is even more pronounced in fancy shapes. An elongated oval might have a dramatically larger face-up appearance than a rounder oval of the same carat weight.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller provides the exact measurements from the grading report and contextualises them. For a round brilliant, they should confirm that the diameter is within the expected range for that carat weight. For fancy shapes, they should discuss the length-to-width ratio and how it affects visual size and finger coverage. A knowledgeable jeweller will understand that face-up area is often more important to buyers than carat weight and will help you optimise accordingly.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller focuses exclusively on carat weight and cannot provide or explain the millimetre measurements, they may not understand diamond proportions at a detailed level, or they may be selling a deeply cut stone where the weight-to-size ratio is unfavourable.

### "If this is a lab-grown diamond, what is the growth method?"

**Why it matters:** Whether a lab-grown diamond was produced by HPHT or CVD can affect its characteristics and, in some cases, its price. CVD diamonds occasionally require post-growth treatment to improve colour, which should be disclosed on the grading report. HPHT diamonds may contain metallic flux inclusions. Neither method is inherently superior, but knowing the growth method is part of understanding what you are purchasing.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller can tell you whether the diamond is HPHT or CVD, explain what that means in practical terms, and point to the disclosure on the grading report. If the diamond has undergone post-growth treatment (such as HPHT annealing to improve colour), the jeweller should disclose this clearly.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller does not know the growth method, cannot find it on the grading report, or dismisses the question as irrelevant, they may lack detailed knowledge of their own inventory. While the growth method is not the most critical factor, a jeweller who cannot answer this question may not be well-versed in the specifics of lab-grown diamonds generally.

## Category 2: Questions About Ring Construction

### "What metal alloy are you using, and why?"

**Why it matters:** Not all gold is the same. 18-karat yellow gold, 18-karat white gold, 18-karat rose gold, 14-karat gold, and platinum all have different properties in terms of hardness, durability, colour stability, and hypoallergenic qualities. The choice of metal affects the ring's longevity, maintenance requirements, and suitability for daily wear.

18-karat white gold, for example, is typically alloyed with palladium or nickel and rhodium-plated to achieve its bright white appearance. The rhodium plating will wear off over time and require reapplication every one to three years, depending on wear patterns. Platinum is naturally white and does not require plating, but it is softer and develops a patina (a matte, slightly brushed surface) that some people love and others find unattractive.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller explains the specific alloy composition, discusses the practical implications for wear and maintenance, and makes a recommendation based on your lifestyle and preferences. They should mention that 18-karat white gold requires periodic rhodium replating, that platinum will patina over time, and that 14-karat gold is more durable (due to higher alloy content) but has a slightly less rich colour than 18-karat. They should ask about any metal allergies, as nickel-based white gold alloys can cause reactions in sensitive individuals.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** Responses like "it is white gold" without further detail, or an inability to discuss alloy composition, suggest a limited understanding of materials. Be particularly cautious if the jeweller cannot tell you whether their white gold uses a nickel or palladium alloy, as this is relevant for anyone with sensitive skin.

### "What is the band thickness and width, and how will it wear over time?"

**Why it matters:** Band dimensions directly affect comfort, durability, and the overall look of the ring. A band that is too thin will feel flimsy, may bend or warp with daily wear, and is more susceptible to damage over years of use. A band that is too thick may feel cumbersome and look disproportionate to a smaller diamond.

For a solitaire engagement ring, a minimum band thickness of 1.6 to 1.8 mm is generally recommended for structural integrity, with 2.0 mm being more robust. Width (the dimension you see from the top) is largely an aesthetic choice, but typically ranges from 1.8 to 2.5 mm for engagement rings.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller provides specific dimensions and explains the trade-offs. They should be able to tell you the band thickness and width for the specific design you are considering, explain how those dimensions affect durability and comfort, and discuss whether the band will be comfort-fit (slightly domed on the inside for easier wearing) or flat. They should also be candid about the long-term implications — a 1.4 mm band in 18-karat gold will eventually wear thin and may need reinforcement or replacement.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller cannot provide specific measurements or says "it is standard thickness," press for numbers. Vague answers may indicate that the ring is being produced to a generic template without careful consideration of structural requirements, or that the band is thinner than ideal to reduce material costs.

### "How are the prongs constructed, and what type of setting is this?"

**Why it matters:** Prongs are what hold your diamond in place. Their construction, size, and number directly affect the security of the stone and the aesthetic of the ring. Poorly constructed prongs are the most common cause of diamond loss from engagement rings.

There are several approaches to prong construction. Prongs may be cast as part of the ring head (single-piece casting), soldered onto a separately fabricated head, or hand-formed by a bench jeweller. Each method has implications for strength, precision, and repairability.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller explains the setting type (four-prong, six-prong, bezel, halo, etc.), the construction method, and the implications. They should be able to discuss why they recommend a particular number of prongs — four prongs show more of the diamond but provide less security than six, for instance — and how the prongs are finished (rounded, pointed, flat-topped, or claw-style). They should also explain how prongs are checked during routine maintenance and what happens if a prong is damaged.

For higher-end custom work, the jeweller should be able to explain whether the head is cast or hand-fabricated, and the advantages of each. Hand-fabricated heads (formed from wire or sheet metal by a bench jeweller) are generally considered superior for strength and precision, but they are more labour-intensive and therefore more expensive.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller cannot explain the construction method or dismisses prong questions as overly technical, they may not have detailed knowledge of the manufacturing process. This is concerning because prong failure is a practical risk, and understanding how the ring is constructed is essential for evaluating its long-term durability.

### "What casting and finishing methods do you use?"

**Why it matters:** Most modern jewellery is produced using lost-wax casting — a process in which a wax model is created, encased in plaster, burned out, and replaced with molten metal. The quality of the casting process affects the density, porosity, and surface quality of the finished ring. Poor casting can result in micro-porosity (tiny air bubbles within the metal) that weakens the structure and creates a rough surface that is difficult to polish to a high standard.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller can describe their manufacturing process in reasonable detail. For custom work, they should explain the progression from CAD (computer-aided design) to wax model or 3D-printed resin model, to casting, to finishing. They should be able to discuss quality control steps — how they check for porosity, how they ensure the setting is properly aligned, and how the ring is finished (hand-polished, machine-polished, or a combination).

For stock or semi-custom rings, the jeweller should know where the ring is manufactured and be able to speak to the quality standards of their supplier.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** Responses like "we use the best methods" or "it is all done by our master jewellers" without specifics may indicate a lack of transparency about the manufacturing process. Some retailers outsource manufacturing to overseas workshops and may not have detailed knowledge of — or control over — the production process.

### "Does the ring come with a structural warranty?"

**Why it matters:** A structural warranty covers manufacturing defects — problems with the ring that arise from how it was made, rather than from wear and tear or accidental damage. This might include prong failure, stone loss due to a defective setting, cracking or breaking of the band due to porosity, or plating failure within an unreasonable timeframe.

A structural warranty is a signal that the jeweller stands behind the quality of their work. The absence of a warranty — or a warranty with significant exclusions — may indicate lower manufacturing standards or a reluctance to assume liability.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller provides a clear, written warranty that specifies what is covered, what is excluded, and for how long. A good structural warranty typically covers manufacturing defects for at least two years and may extend to a lifetime warranty with conditions (such as regular maintenance checks). The jeweller should explain what is and is not covered — for example, a warranty might cover prong failure due to a casting defect but not prong wear from daily use, which is a maintenance issue.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller offers only a verbal assurance ("we will look after you"), has no written warranty, or presents a warranty document filled with exclusions that effectively render it meaningless, proceed with caution. A reputable jeweller should be willing to put their commitment to quality in writing.

## Category 3: Questions About Pricing

### "Can I see an itemised breakdown of the price?"

**Why it matters:** An engagement ring price typically comprises the cost of the diamond (or diamonds), the cost of the metal and setting, the cost of labour (design and manufacturing), and the jeweller's margin. Understanding how the total price is constructed helps you evaluate whether the pricing is fair and where there may be room for negotiation or optimisation.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller provides a clear breakdown showing the cost of the centre stone, any side stones, the setting (materials and labour), and any additional costs such as engraving, sizing, or certification. They should be able to explain how the diamond price relates to its grading — why a G colour VS2 at that carat weight costs what it does, and how the price would change if you adjusted the specifications.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller provides only a total price and is unwilling to break it down, you have no way to assess whether the diamond is priced competitively, whether the setting cost is reasonable, or where savings might be available. Some jewellers resist itemisation because it allows comparison shopping. While we understand this commercially, it works against your interests as a buyer. A jeweller who values transparency over information asymmetry is demonstrating confidence in their pricing.

### "How does this price compare to the market, and how do you justify it?"

**Why it matters:** Diamond prices are not arbitrary — there are well-established wholesale price lists (Rapaport for natural diamonds, various benchmarks for lab-grown) that the industry uses as reference points. While retail prices include legitimate markups for expertise, service, quality assurance, and overhead, the underlying diamond cost should bear a reasonable relationship to market rates.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller can contextualise their pricing. They might explain that their diamond prices reflect a certain percentage above wholesale, that their setting costs reflect the quality of their manufacturing process, or that their prices include services (such as a free first-year maintenance check) that other jewellers charge for separately. They should not be offended by the question. A confident jeweller knows their pricing is fair and is comfortable explaining why.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** Responses like "you are paying for the experience" or "our diamonds are specially selected" without specifics may indicate pricing that relies on information asymmetry rather than genuine value. Be cautious of jewellers who become defensive or dismissive when asked about pricing methodology.

### "Are there any additional costs I should know about?"

**Why it matters:** The quoted price for an engagement ring may not include everything you need. Additional costs might include: ring sizing, rhodium replating (for white gold), engraving, insurance valuation or appraisal, packaging, shipping, or future maintenance. These costs can add several hundred dollars to the total and should be disclosed upfront.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller proactively lists any additional costs before you ask, or provides a comprehensive answer when prompted. They should be specific — "ring sizing is included in the price," "rhodium replating costs $80 and is recommended annually," "we provide a complimentary insurance valuation." Complete transparency about the total cost of ownership is a hallmark of a reputable jeweller.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If additional costs emerge late in the process — at the point of payment or after the ring has been completed — this suggests either disorganisation or a deliberate strategy to anchor you to the initial price before revealing supplementary charges. Neither reflects well on the jeweller.

### "What is your return and exchange policy?"

**Why it matters:** Despite your best efforts, there is always a possibility that the ring will not be right — your partner may prefer a different style, the diamond may not look as expected in person, or your circumstances may change. Understanding the return and exchange policy before purchase protects you against these eventualities.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller has a clearly documented return and exchange policy. For stock items, a 14 to 30 day return window is standard. For custom-made rings, returns may be more limited (the ring was made to your specifications, after all), but a reputable jeweller should offer an exchange or store credit option. The policy should be provided in writing and should specify any conditions — whether the ring must be unworn, whether custom items are exchange-only, and whether there is a restocking fee.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller has no written policy, says "we will work something out if there is a problem," or imposes conditions that effectively prevent returns (such as a very short window or an excessive restocking fee), you are assuming all of the risk. This is not reasonable for a purchase of this significance.

## Category 4: Questions About the Process

### "Can I see examples of similar rings you have made?"

**Why it matters:** Past work is the best predictor of future quality. Seeing finished rings — particularly in the specific style, setting type, and price range you are considering — gives you a tangible sense of the jeweller's craftsmanship, design aesthetic, and attention to detail.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller has a portfolio of previous work, ideally showing high-resolution photographs from multiple angles, including close-up shots of settings, prongs, and finishing details. Even better if they can show you finished rings in person. They should be able to discuss specific design decisions in their past work — why they chose a particular prong style, how they addressed a structural challenge, or how they matched side stones to a centre diamond.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller has no portfolio, uses only stock photographs from suppliers or manufacturers, or cannot show you work similar to what you are commissioning, they may not have the experience to deliver what you want. This is especially important for custom designs, where the gap between concept and execution can be significant.

### "What is the timeline for a custom ring, and what are the stages?"

**Why it matters:** A custom engagement ring typically takes four to eight weeks to complete, depending on complexity and the jeweller's workload. Understanding the stages — consultation, design, CAD rendering, approval, wax model (if applicable), casting, setting, finishing, and quality inspection — helps you plan your timeline and set realistic expectations.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller provides a clear, stage-by-stage timeline with approximate dates for key milestones. They explain when you will have opportunities to review and approve the design, how changes are handled at each stage, and what happens if there are delays (for example, if the diamond you ordered needs to be recut or exchanged). They should be realistic about timelines rather than promising faster delivery than they can reliably achieve.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** Responses like "it depends" or "usually a few weeks" without a specific breakdown suggest either a lack of process discipline or an unwillingness to commit to a timeline. If the jeweller cannot articulate their own manufacturing process, they may not have full control over it — which increases the risk of delays, miscommunication, or quality issues.

### "How many design revisions are included?"

**Why it matters:** Custom ring design is iterative. Your first CAD rendering may not be exactly what you envisioned, and adjustments are normal and expected. However, unlimited revisions can lead to scope creep and delays, while too few may leave you with a design you are not fully satisfied with.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller specifies how many rounds of revisions are included in the quoted price — typically two to three — and explains the cost of additional revisions if needed. They should set clear expectations about what constitutes a revision (a change to prong style) versus a fundamental redesign (a completely different setting type), as the latter may require a new quotation.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller says "as many as you need" without qualification, they may be underestimating the complexity of the design process or may adjust the price later if the project takes longer than expected. Conversely, if they allow only one revision or charge for changes from the outset, they may be prioritising throughput over customer satisfaction.

### "What is your satisfaction guarantee?"

**Why it matters:** This question tests the jeweller's confidence in their own work. A satisfaction guarantee is a commitment to resolve any issues with the finished ring — whether it is a manufacturing defect, a sizing problem, or a discrepancy between the approved design and the delivered product.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller describes a clear process for handling dissatisfaction. This might include a commitment to remake the ring if it does not match the approved design, a free resize within a specified period, or a credit toward a different design if the finished ring is not what the buyer expected. The key is specificity — a vague promise to "make it right" is less reassuring than a defined process.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller has no satisfaction guarantee, qualifies it with extensive exceptions, or becomes defensive when the question is asked, they may not be fully confident in their ability to deliver consistent quality. This is a significant concern for custom work, where the risk of misalignment between expectation and delivery is inherently higher.

## Category 5: Questions About Aftercare

### "What maintenance does this ring need, and how often?"

**Why it matters:** An engagement ring is worn daily, often for decades. It requires regular maintenance to remain in good condition and to ensure the diamond remains securely set. Understanding the maintenance requirements before purchase helps you plan for the ongoing cost of ownership and avoid preventable damage.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller provides specific maintenance recommendations, such as:

- **Prong inspection:** Every 6 to 12 months. Prongs wear down over time and can become thin, loose, or bent, increasing the risk of stone loss.
- **Professional cleaning:** Every 6 to 12 months, though more frequent home cleaning with mild soap and warm water is fine.
- **Rhodium replating (white gold only):** Every 12 to 24 months, depending on wear.
- **General inspection:** Annual check of the band for wear, thinning, or deformation.

The jeweller should explain what each service involves, how long it takes, and what it costs. Some jewellers include complimentary maintenance for the first year or for the lifetime of the ring; others charge per service.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller says the ring is "maintenance-free" or "will last forever without any care," they are either uninformed or misleading you. All fine jewellery requires maintenance. A jeweller who downplays this is setting you up for problems down the line — and potentially for the devastating experience of losing a diamond from a worn prong that should have been caught during a routine inspection.

### "How often should prongs be checked, and what does that involve?"

**Why it matters:** This is arguably the single most important aftercare question. Prong failure is the primary cause of diamond loss from engagement rings, and it is almost entirely preventable with regular inspection. Prongs should be checked by a jeweller every six to twelve months. The inspection involves examining each prong under magnification for signs of wear, thinning, lifting, or damage, and applying gentle pressure to ensure the diamond is secure.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller recommends a specific inspection interval (six months is ideal, twelve months is acceptable) and explains what the inspection involves. They should tell you the cost (many jewellers offer complimentary prong checks for rings they have sold) and describe what happens if a problem is found — typically, the prong is retipped (rebuilt) or the head is replaced, which can be done while you wait or within a few days.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller does not mention prong checks or suggests they are unnecessary, this is a serious concern. Prong wear is not optional — it happens to every ring that is worn regularly, and the consequences of ignoring it (losing your diamond) are severe and not typically covered by insurance without evidence of regular maintenance.

### "What does the warranty cover, and what is excluded?"

**Why it matters:** A warranty is only as good as its terms. Understanding precisely what is covered — and equally importantly, what is not covered — prevents unpleasant surprises when something goes wrong. The distinction between manufacturing defects (covered) and normal wear and tear (not covered) is often the source of disputes between buyers and jewellers.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller provides a written warranty document that clearly defines:

- **What is covered:** Manufacturing defects in casting, setting, soldering, and finishing. Stone loss due to a defective setting (within a reasonable period). Structural failure of the band or head.
- **What is not covered:** Normal wear and tear (band thinning, prong wear, scratching). Accidental damage (impact, crushing, chemical exposure). Damage resulting from failure to maintain the ring (such as stone loss from prongs that were not checked as recommended).
- **Duration:** A minimum of two years for manufacturing defects, with some jewellers offering lifetime structural warranties conditional on regular maintenance.
- **Conditions:** Any requirements you must meet to maintain warranty coverage, such as annual prong inspections or avoiding exposure to harsh chemicals.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the warranty is verbal only, excessively vague ("we will take care of any problems"), or laden with exclusions that effectively nullify the coverage, the jeweller is not providing meaningful protection. A written warranty with specific terms is a reasonable expectation for any purchase of this value.

### "What cleaning and polishing can I do at home, and what should I leave to you?"

**Why it matters:** Regular cleaning keeps a diamond sparkling and allows you to spot potential issues (such as a loose stone or a bent prong) early. However, improper cleaning methods can damage certain metals or settings, and polishing should generally be left to professionals.

**What a good answer looks like:** The jeweller provides clear home care instructions, such as:

- **Safe home cleaning:** Soak the ring in warm water with a few drops of mild dish soap for 20 to 30 minutes, then gently brush with a very soft toothbrush (baby toothbrush or similar), paying attention to the underside of the diamond where oils and dirt accumulate. Rinse in warm water and pat dry with a lint-free cloth.
- **What to avoid:** Harsh chemicals (bleach, chlorine, acetone), ultrasonic cleaners (which can loosen stones in certain settings), abrasive materials, and steam cleaning at home.
- **When to bring it in:** For professional polishing to remove scratches, deep cleaning using professional-grade equipment, prong inspection, and rhodium replating.

**What an evasive answer indicates:** If the jeweller simply says "bring it in for cleaning" without providing home care instructions, they may be prioritising service revenue over customer education. While professional cleaning is valuable, basic home care between professional services is both practical and beneficial, and a good jeweller will equip you with the knowledge to do it properly.

The questions in this guide are not adversarial. They are the questions a knowledgeable buyer asks — and a confident, competent jeweller welcomes.

Your engagement ring is one of the most significant purchases you will make. You deserve to make it with your eyes open.

# Section 6: Realistic Budget Expectations

## The "Two to Three Months' Salary" Rule Is Marketing, Not Advice

The idea that you should spend two to three months' salary on an engagement ring is one of the most successful advertising campaigns in modern history. It was invented by the De Beers diamond cartel in the 1930s and 1940s as part of a broader strategy to increase diamond demand. In the United States, the original suggestion was one month's salary. By the 1980s, De Beers had pushed it to two months. In some markets, including Japan, the figure quoted was three months.

This "rule" has no basis in financial planning, relationship quality, or gemological value. It was designed to sell more expensive diamonds. Nothing more.

What Australians actually spend varies enormously. Industry data suggests the median engagement ring purchase in Australia falls between $5,000 and $8,000 AUD. Some couples spend $2,000. Others spend $50,000. Neither figure is inherently right or wrong. What matters is that you spend an amount you can genuinely afford, on a ring that is well-made and represents genuine value for money.

The best engagement ring is one that does not create financial stress. Starting a marriage with consumer debt from a ring purchase is a poor foundation. If you have savings earmarked for a home deposit, a wedding, or an emergency fund, raiding those accounts for a larger diamond is not a sound decision. A well-chosen $4,000 ring can be every bit as beautiful and meaningful as a $20,000 ring -- and it will not keep you awake at night worrying about the credit card bill.

Throughout this section, we will break down where the money actually goes when you purchase an engagement ring, what you can realistically expect at various price points, and how to allocate your budget for maximum quality and value.


## What Actually Drives the Price of an Engagement Ring

Understanding cost breakdown is essential to making an informed decision. An engagement ring has two primary cost components: the diamond (or centre stone) and the setting (the metal ring, its construction, and any accent stones).

### The Diamond: 60 to 75 Per Cent of Total Cost

In most engagement rings, the centre diamond represents the majority of the cost. For a natural diamond, this proportion is typically 65 to 75 per cent. For lab-grown diamonds, the proportion is lower -- often 40 to 55 per cent -- because lab-grown stones cost significantly less per carat.

The price of a diamond is determined by the Four Cs (cut, colour, clarity, and carat weight), but not equally. Carat weight has the single largest impact on price because diamond pricing operates on a per-carat basis with price jumps at popular weight thresholds. A 1.00 carat diamond does not cost twice as much as a 0.50 carat diamond of identical quality -- it may cost three to four times as much, because larger rough diamonds are exponentially rarer.

Cut quality has the second-largest impact, though many consumers underestimate this. An Excellent or Ideal cut diamond will command a premium over a Good cut stone of the same weight, colour, and clarity. This premium is entirely justified -- cut quality is the single most important factor in how beautiful a diamond looks to the naked eye.

Colour and clarity affect price in a more linear fashion. Moving one grade in either category typically shifts the price by 10 to 20 per cent. The key insight is that several grades of colour and clarity are visually indistinguishable to the naked eye, which creates opportunities to optimise your budget without sacrificing appearance.

### The Setting: 25 to 40 Per Cent of Total Cost

The setting includes the metal (gold or platinum), the design and construction of the ring, any accent diamonds or gemstones, and the labour involved in manufacturing. Many consumers focus almost exclusively on the centre diamond and treat the setting as an afterthought. This is a mistake.

A poorly constructed setting can result in a lost diamond, uncomfortable daily wear, excessive maintenance costs, and premature failure of the ring. The setting is what you wear every day. It needs to be robust, well-finished, and properly engineered.

For a simple solitaire in 18-karat white gold, the setting cost (including manufacturing labour) typically ranges from $1,200 to $2,500 AUD depending on quality of construction and the jeweller's pricing model. A halo setting with pave accent diamonds will cost more -- typically $2,000 to $4,500 -- because of the additional diamonds and the more complex setting work required.

Platinum settings cost approximately 30 to 50 per cent more than equivalent 18-karat gold settings, primarily because platinum is denser (requiring more metal by weight), harder to work with (requiring more labour), and currently priced higher as a raw material.


## Realistic Price Ranges in Australian Dollars

The following ranges reflect what you can genuinely expect to receive at each price point from a reputable jeweller in Australia. These are based on current market pricing for well-cut, independently certified diamonds in quality settings.

### Budget Range: $2,000 to $5,000 AUD

At this price point, you are working with real constraints, but a beautiful ring is absolutely achievable. This is where lab-grown diamonds offer a transformative advantage.

**With a natural diamond**, your centre stone at this budget will typically be 0.30 to 0.60 carats. At the lower end of this range, you may be limited to a 0.30 carat stone in a simple setting. At $5,000, a well-chosen 0.50 to 0.60 carat natural diamond in a solitaire or simple halo setting is realistic.

**With a lab-grown diamond**, the same budget opens up significantly. A 1.00 to 1.50 carat lab-grown diamond with excellent cut quality in a well-made 18-karat gold setting is achievable at $3,500 to $5,000. This represents a dramatic difference in visual impact for the same expenditure.

**What NOT to compromise on at this budget:**

- **Cut quality.** Never drop below Very Good cut to save money. A well-cut 0.40 carat diamond will look better than a poorly cut 0.60 carat diamond.
- **Setting quality.** Do not accept a flimsy setting to afford a slightly larger stone. A lost diamond is far more expensive than a slightly smaller one.
- **Independent certification.** Every diamond, regardless of price, should come with a certificate from GIA, AGS, or IGI (for lab-grown). If a jeweller offers an uncertified stone at this price point to "save you money," walk away.

**Where you can save at this budget:**

- Colour: G or H colour diamonds are visually white in most settings and cost significantly less than D, E, or F grades.
- Clarity: SI1 clarity is usually eye-clean and costs far less than VS2 or higher.
- Carat weight: Buy just under popular thresholds (0.48 instead of 0.50, 0.90 instead of 1.00) for meaningful savings.

### Mid-Range: $5,000 to $12,000 AUD

This is where most Australian engagement ring purchases fall, and for good reason. This budget provides access to genuinely high-quality rings without requiring financial strain for most dual-income couples.

**With a natural diamond**, expect a centre stone of 0.60 to 1.00 carats with good to very good specifications across all Four Cs. At $8,000 to $10,000, a well-cut 0.80 to 0.90 carat natural diamond (G-H colour, VS2-SI1 clarity) in a quality 18-karat gold or platinum solitaire setting is a realistic and excellent purchase. At $12,000, you may reach a 1.00 carat natural diamond with strong specifications, though you will likely need to make minor compromises in colour or clarity to stay within budget.

**With a lab-grown diamond**, this budget provides access to stones that would cost $25,000 to $50,000 or more as natural diamonds. A 1.50 to 2.50 carat lab-grown diamond with excellent cut, near-colourless grade, and high clarity in a premium setting is achievable. This is the price range where lab-grown diamonds offer perhaps the most compelling value proposition -- the visual result is genuinely spectacular.

**Quality indicators at this budget:**

- Settings should be well-finished with no rough surfaces, consistent prong work, and comfortable fit.
- You should have meaningful choice in diamond specifications rather than being limited to whatever is available.
- The jeweller should be able to show you multiple stone options and explain the trade-offs between them.
- Custom or semi-custom design work becomes accessible, particularly at the upper end of this range.

### Premium Range: $12,000 to $25,000 AUD

At this level, you are purchasing a ring that represents genuinely high quality across all components. The improvements over the mid-range are real, though they follow the law of diminishing returns -- the jump in quality from $5,000 to $12,000 is more dramatic than the jump from $12,000 to $25,000.

**With a natural diamond**, expect a centre stone of 1.00 to 1.50 carats with strong specifications. At $15,000 to $20,000, a 1.00 to 1.20 carat diamond with Excellent cut, F-G colour, and VS1-VS2 clarity in a premium platinum or gold setting is realistic. At $25,000, a 1.30 to 1.50 carat stone with similar specifications is achievable.

**Where the additional money goes:**

- Higher colour grades (D-F) that offer subtle but genuine visual whiteness, particularly in platinum or white gold settings.
- Higher clarity grades (VVS2 to VS1) that, while not visible to the naked eye, provide peace of mind and stronger long-term value retention for natural diamonds.
- Premium setting construction: heavier shanks, more refined finishing, hand-set accent diamonds, and design details that elevate the overall appearance.
- More complex designs: intricate halo settings, channel-set bands, or bespoke designs with hand-engraving or milgrain detailing.

### Luxury Range: $25,000 and Above

This range is justified when you are purchasing exceptional stones with rare characteristics, or commissioning truly bespoke designs from master craftspeople.

**What justifies this price:**

- **Exceptional natural diamonds**: 1.50 carats and above with top-tier specifications (D-E colour, VVS clarity, Excellent/Ideal cut) from reputable mines with full provenance documentation.
- **Rare fancy-coloured diamonds**: Natural pink, blue, or green diamonds are exponentially rarer than white diamonds and command prices that reflect genuine geological scarcity.
- **Master-level bespoke design**: Rings conceived and executed by experienced designer-jewellers, involving hand carving, custom wax models, multiple design consultations, and finishing work that may take weeks or months.
- **Important accent stones**: Side stones or surrounding diamonds that are individually selected and matched, rather than purchased in bulk parcels.

**What does NOT justify this price:**

- A brand name. If a significant portion of the price is attributable to the jeweller's name on the box rather than the quality of the materials and workmanship, you are paying for marketing, not quality.
- Artificial scarcity narratives. Claims that a particular diamond is "rare" or "one of a kind" should be supported by its grading report, not by sales rhetoric.
- Elaborate packaging and showroom experience. These amenities are pleasant, but they should not add thousands of dollars to the price of the ring itself.


## Budget Allocation: The 60/40 Framework

A practical framework for allocating your engagement ring budget is the 60/40 split: approximately 60 per cent of your total budget on the centre diamond, and 40 per cent on the setting, accent stones (if any), and manufacturing.

This ratio is a guideline, not a rigid rule. It shifts depending on your priorities:

- **If maximum visual size is your priority**, you might allocate 70 per cent to the diamond and 30 per cent to a simpler setting (a classic solitaire or a plain band with four or six prongs).
- **If design complexity is your priority**, a 50/50 split allows for a slightly smaller centre stone in a more elaborate setting with accent diamonds, hand-engraving, or custom design elements.
- **If durability is your priority**, allocating more to a platinum setting with robust construction may be worth accepting a marginally smaller centre stone.

### Prioritise Cut Above All Else

Within your diamond budget, allocate the largest share to securing excellent cut quality. This is the single most impactful decision you will make. A well-cut diamond maximises light return, creating the brilliance and fire that make a diamond visually stunning. A poorly cut diamond -- even one with high colour and clarity grades -- will look dull and lifeless.

Practically, this means: if your budget forces a choice between a 1.00 carat diamond with Good cut and a 0.85 carat diamond with Excellent cut, choose the smaller stone every time. The visual difference in cut quality is dramatic and immediately apparent. The visual difference between 0.85 and 1.00 carats is subtle and requires side-by-side comparison to notice.

### Do Not Overspend on Colour and Clarity

Colour grades D through F are considered "colourless." Grades G and H are "near-colourless." In most ring settings -- particularly white gold and platinum -- G and H colour diamonds face up white to the naked eye. The difference between a D and a G is detectable under controlled laboratory conditions with comparison stones. It is rarely noticeable in a ring on someone's finger in normal lighting.

Similarly, clarity grades VS2 and SI1 are almost always eye-clean (no inclusions visible without magnification). Paying 30 to 40 per cent more for a VVS1 grade provides no visible improvement in daily wear. The only practical reason to pay for higher clarity is if long-term value retention of a natural diamond is a priority, as higher-clarity stones historically hold their value slightly better.

### Do Not Underspend on the Ring Itself

A common mistake is spending the entire budget on the largest possible diamond and fitting it into the cheapest available setting. This creates problems:

- **Thin bands** (under 1.5mm) are prone to bending, warping, and eventual cracking. They feel insubstantial on the finger and are difficult to resize.
- **Poorly set prongs** increase the risk of the centre stone becoming loose or falling out entirely. Re-setting a diamond after loss is not possible -- you must purchase a new stone.
- **Rough finishing** on the inside of the band causes discomfort and skin irritation during daily wear.
- **Low-quality accent stones** in halo or pave settings may be poorly matched, visibly included, or set unevenly, undermining the overall appearance of the ring.

Invest in the setting. You will wear it every day for decades. It needs to be well-made.


## Where Retail Markup Goes

Understanding retail pricing helps you evaluate whether you are receiving fair value. A typical mall or high-street jeweller operates with a markup of 2.0 to 2.5 times on diamonds and 2.5 to 3.5 times on finished rings. This means a ring retailing for $10,000 may contain $4,000 to $5,000 in materials and direct labour costs. The remaining $5,000 to $6,000 covers:

- **Retail rent**: Prime shopping centre or high-street locations cost $150,000 to $500,000 or more per year in lease costs in Australian capital cities. This is often the single largest overhead expense.
- **Staff costs**: Sales associates, managers, security, and administrative staff. A single retail store may employ four to eight people.
- **Marketing and advertising**: Digital advertising, print media, social media management, search engine marketing, and brand campaigns. Large jewellery retailers spend 8 to 15 per cent of revenue on marketing.
- **Brand premium**: For internationally recognised brands, a significant portion of the price reflects the brand name itself. You are paying for the logo on the box and the perceived status associated with the brand.
- **Inventory carrying costs**: A retail jeweller may hold $500,000 to $2,000,000 in diamond and ring inventory at any given time. The cost of financing this inventory is passed on to the consumer.
- **Insurance, compliance, and overheads**: Business insurance, security systems, point-of-sale systems, accounting, legal compliance, and general administration.

None of these costs are illegitimate. Running a retail jewellery business is expensive. But it is important to understand that when you purchase from a large retailer, a substantial portion of your payment covers business overheads rather than the physical ring on your finger.

**Private jewellers and workshop-based businesses** operate with significantly lower overheads. Without prime retail rent, large staff counts, and national marketing campaigns, these businesses can offer comparable or superior quality at lower prices, or significantly better quality at comparable prices. The trade-off is that you may not have a glossy showroom experience, and you may need to make an appointment rather than walking in off the street. For many buyers, this trade-off is well worth it.

When comparing quotes from different jewellers, always ask for itemised pricing that separates the cost of the diamond from the cost of the setting and manufacturing. This allows direct comparison and reveals where each jeweller's margin lies.


## Hidden and Ongoing Costs

The purchase price of an engagement ring is not the total cost of ownership. Several ongoing expenses should be factored into your financial planning.

### Insurance: $150 to $400 Per Year

Engagement ring insurance is strongly recommended. Rings are worn daily in all conditions and are vulnerable to loss, theft, and accidental damage. Most home and contents insurance policies can be extended to cover specified jewellery items, or you can purchase standalone jewellery insurance.

Premiums are typically calculated as 1.5 to 3.0 per cent of the ring's appraised value per year. A $10,000 ring will cost approximately $150 to $300 per year to insure. You will need a formal valuation (not the same as the purchase receipt) for insurance purposes, which costs $50 to $150 from a qualified valuer.

Revaluation is recommended every two to three years, as replacement costs change with market conditions. Insist on "agreed value" or "replacement value" cover rather than "market value" cover, as the latter may not cover the full cost of replacing the ring with one of equivalent quality.

### Rhodium Replating: Every 12 to 24 Months (White Gold Only)

White gold is naturally a pale yellow colour. The bright white appearance of white gold jewellery comes from a thin layer of rhodium plating applied to the surface. This plating wears away with daily use, typically within 12 to 24 months, revealing the warmer colour beneath.

Rhodium replating costs $80 to $150 per application and is a routine maintenance procedure that takes 30 to 60 minutes. It is not a defect -- it is a normal characteristic of white gold. If you prefer to avoid this ongoing maintenance, consider platinum (which is naturally white and does not require plating) or yellow gold (which does not require plating because the natural colour is the desired colour).

### Prong Re-Tipping: Every 5 to 10 Years

The prongs that hold your centre diamond in place gradually wear down through daily contact with surfaces, fabrics, and skin. Over five to ten years, prongs may wear thin enough to compromise the security of the stone. Prong re-tipping or rebuilding costs $100 to $300 depending on the number of prongs and the metal type, and should be performed before the prongs become dangerously thin.

Have your prongs inspected annually by a qualified jeweller. This inspection is often complimentary and takes only a few minutes. It is far less expensive to re-tip a worn prong than to replace a lost diamond.

### Professional Cleaning: Every 6 to 12 Months

Diamonds attract oils and grime from skin contact, hand washing, cooking, and cosmetics. Over time, this buildup dulls the diamond's brilliance. Professional ultrasonic cleaning restores the stone's appearance and is typically offered free or at nominal cost ($20 to $50) by most jewellers.

Between professional cleanings, you can maintain your ring at home by soaking it in warm water with a small amount of mild dishwashing liquid, then gently brushing with a soft toothbrush. Avoid harsh chemicals, abrasive cleaners, and ultrasonic cleaners not specifically designed for jewellery.

### Resizing: $80 to $250

Finger size changes over time due to weight fluctuation, pregnancy, aging, and seasonal temperature variation. Most rings can be resized up or down by one to two sizes. Resizing costs $80 to $250 depending on the ring's design and metal type. Rings with continuous pave settings, channel-set stones, or eternity bands are more difficult and expensive to resize, and some cannot be resized at all.

### Wedding Band Fitting: $50 to $200

When you purchase a wedding band, you may want it shaped or contoured to sit flush against your engagement ring. This is a separate cost from the wedding band itself and involves either selecting a pre-shaped band or having a straight band modified. Budget $50 to $200 for this service.


## Getting the Best Value for Your Budget

### Buy Just Under Popular Carat Weights

Diamond prices increase significantly at popular weight thresholds: 0.50, 0.75, 1.00, 1.50, and 2.00 carats. A 0.99 carat diamond is visually indistinguishable from a 1.00 carat diamond -- even an experienced gemologist cannot tell the difference by looking at the face-up appearance. Yet the 1.00 carat stone may cost 15 to 20 per cent more simply because of the psychological significance of the "one carat" threshold.

Search specifically for diamonds at 0.48 to 0.49, 0.70 to 0.74, 0.90 to 0.99, and 1.45 to 1.49 carats. The savings are real and the visual difference is nonexistent.

### Prioritise Cut Over Everything Else

We have emphasised this throughout this guide because it cannot be overstated. Cut quality is the single most important factor in a diamond's visual beauty. It determines how much light enters the stone, how efficiently that light is reflected back to the viewer, and how much fire (spectral dispersion) the diamond displays.

Two diamonds of identical carat weight, colour, and clarity can look dramatically different if one has a superior cut. The well-cut stone will appear larger, brighter, and more alive. The poorly cut stone will look dark, dull, and lifeless despite having identical "paper" specifications.

### Consider Lab-Grown Diamonds

Lab-grown diamonds are chemically, physically, and optically identical to natural diamonds. They are real diamonds, created in controlled laboratory conditions rather than extracted from the earth. They cost 70 to 85 per cent less than natural diamonds of equivalent specifications.

If your priority is the size, beauty, and quality of the diamond you wear rather than geological origin, lab-grown diamonds represent exceptional value. A budget of $5,000 spent on a lab-grown diamond will purchase a stone that would cost $15,000 to $25,000 as a natural diamond.

The trade-off is resale value. Lab-grown diamonds have minimal resale value and their prices have been declining as production capacity increases. Natural diamonds retain some resale value, though typically far less than consumers expect (30 to 50 per cent of retail price in most cases). If you are purchasing a diamond as a financial asset, neither natural nor lab-grown diamonds are good investments. If you are purchasing a diamond to wear and enjoy, lab-grown diamonds offer outstanding value.

### Compare Itemised Quotes

Request itemised quotes from at least three jewellers before making a purchase. An itemised quote should separately list:

- The cost of the centre diamond (with full grading specifications)
- The cost of the setting (metal type, weight, design)
- The cost of any accent diamonds
- Manufacturing or labour charges
- Any additional fees (certification, valuation, engraving)

This transparency allows you to make direct comparisons. A jeweller who refuses to provide itemised pricing may be obscuring excessive margins. Walk away from any jeweller who will not tell you exactly what you are paying for.

### Negotiate -- But Know What Is Negotiable

Diamond pricing is more flexible than most consumers realise. Margins on diamonds are typically 15 to 50 per cent above wholesale cost, depending on the retailer. There is usually room for a 5 to 15 per cent discount, particularly on natural diamonds and particularly if you are paying in full rather than on a payment plan.

Setting costs are less negotiable because they represent more fixed costs (metal, labour, overheads). However, a jeweller may offer value additions such as complimentary ring cleaning, free resizing within a certain period, or waived engraving fees.

What should never be negotiated downward is quality. A discount achieved by substituting a lower-quality diamond, using thinner metal, or cutting corners on construction is not a discount -- it is a worse product at a lower price.

# Section 7: Red Flags Checklist

## Why This Section Exists

The jewellery industry, like any industry involving high-value purchases and information asymmetry, contains businesses and individuals who take advantage of consumer inexperience. Most jewellers are honest professionals who take pride in their work. But some are not, and the difference between a fair deal and a poor one can amount to thousands of dollars.

This section is designed to help you identify warning signs before you commit to a purchase. None of these red flags in isolation necessarily indicates a dishonest business -- context matters. But if you encounter several of them in a single transaction, you should proceed with extreme caution or walk away entirely.


## Red Flags in the Sales Process

### High-Pressure Tactics

Any jeweller who creates artificial urgency is employing a manipulation technique. Common forms include:

- **"This diamond won't be here tomorrow."** Diamonds are a commodity. Unless you are looking at a genuinely rare fancy-coloured diamond or a specific antique piece, another stone of equivalent specifications can be sourced. A jeweller who tells you that a round brilliant white diamond is "the last one" is being dishonest.
- **"I can only hold this price until end of day."** Legitimate pricing does not expire in hours. If a jeweller's pricing changes daily, their pricing is arbitrary.
- **"Another customer is looking at this exact ring."** This may or may not be true, but it is irrelevant. Your purchase should be based on whether the ring is right for you, not on competitive anxiety.
- **"Today-only discounts" or "sale ends tonight."** Perpetual sales are a hallmark of businesses that inflate prices to create the illusion of discounts. If a jeweller is always running a sale, nothing is ever actually on sale.

A reputable jeweller understands that an engagement ring is a significant purchase that deserves careful consideration. They will encourage you to take your time, compare options, and return when you are ready. Pressure to buy immediately is a reliable indicator that the deal benefits the seller far more than the buyer.

### No Independent Certification

Every diamond sold at retail should be accompanied by a grading report from an independent gemological laboratory. The most respected laboratories are:

- **GIA** (Gemological Institute of America) -- widely considered the industry standard for natural diamonds
- **AGS** (American Gem Society) -- particularly respected for cut grading
- **IGI** (International Gemological Institute) -- widely used for lab-grown diamonds and increasingly accepted for natural stones

If a jeweller offers a diamond without independent certification, or with certification only from their own "in-house" laboratory, this is a significant red flag. In-house grading is inherently conflicted -- the seller is also the grader. Without independent verification, you have no way to confirm that the diamond's specifications are as described.

Some jewellers will offer to have the diamond certified "after purchase" at your expense. This is backwards. Certification should exist before the sale, and the cost should be absorbed by the seller.

### Vague or Evasive Answers

When you ask specific questions about a diamond's specifications, origin, or the construction details of a ring, you should receive specific answers. Red flags include:

- Responding to technical questions with emotional deflections: "Don't worry about the numbers -- look how beautiful it sparkles!"
- Inability to explain what a diamond's grading report means
- Reluctance to discuss where the diamond was sourced or how the ring was manufactured
- Answering questions about price with "it depends" without then explaining what it depends on
- Refusing to put specifications or promises in writing

A knowledgeable, honest jeweller will welcome technical questions. They will explain specifications clearly, acknowledge trade-offs honestly, and put everything in writing without hesitation.

### No Itemised Pricing

If a jeweller quotes only a total price for the completed ring without breaking down the cost of the diamond, the setting, and the labour, you cannot evaluate whether the price is fair. Itemised pricing is standard practice among reputable jewellers. Refusal to itemise suggests that the margins on individual components would not withstand scrutiny.

### Commission-Based Steering

In larger retail jewellery stores, sales staff may earn commission that varies by product category or margin. This creates an incentive to steer you toward products that earn the salesperson more money, rather than products that best suit your needs and budget.

Signs of commission steering include:

- Consistently being shown options above your stated budget
- Dismissing lab-grown diamonds without acknowledging their legitimate advantages
- Pushing specific brands or product lines while disparaging alternatives
- Pivoting away from items you express interest in toward more expensive alternatives

### "Too Good to Be True" Pricing

If one jeweller's price is dramatically lower than every other quote you have received for an apparently equivalent product, investigate why. Possible explanations include:

- The diamond's grading has been inflated (the stone is lower quality than claimed)
- The setting is made with inferior construction methods or thinner metal
- The diamond is treated or enhanced in ways that have not been disclosed
- The grading certificate is from a laboratory with relaxed standards
- The business operates on volume with legitimate lower margins (this is possible, but verify)

Genuine bargains exist, but they are rarely 40 to 50 per cent below market price. At that discount level, something is almost always being misrepresented.


## Red Flags in the Diamond Itself

### No Laboratory Certificate

As discussed above, an uncertified diamond is an unverified diamond. You are relying entirely on the seller's word regarding its specifications. Even well-intentioned jewellers can make errors in grading -- and less scrupulous ones will deliberately overgrade stones to justify higher prices.

### Certification from Unknown or Disreputable Laboratories

Not all grading laboratories apply the same standards. Some laboratories are known for "generous" grading -- consistently assigning higher colour and clarity grades than GIA or AGS would for the same stone. A diamond graded as G colour, VS2 clarity by a lenient laboratory might receive an I colour, SI1 grade from GIA. The physical diamond is the same, but the inflated grading allows it to be sold at a higher price.

If you are unfamiliar with the laboratory named on a grading report, research it before accepting the grade at face value. Stick with GIA, AGS, or IGI for the highest confidence in grading accuracy.

### "Investment Grade" Claims

Diamonds are not investments. They are consumer goods that lose a significant portion of their retail value the moment they are purchased, much like a new car driven off the lot. A natural diamond purchased at retail for $10,000 might sell on the secondary market for $3,000 to $5,000 -- and that is for a stone with strong specifications and full documentation.

Any jeweller who describes a diamond as "investment grade," implies it will appreciate in value, or suggests it is a sound financial investment is either uninformed or dishonest. Diamonds can hold sentimental and aesthetic value for a lifetime. They are not financial instruments.

### Certificate vs. Visual Discrepancy

If a diamond's grading report says it is G colour, VS2 clarity with Excellent cut, but the stone looks yellowish, has visible inclusions, or appears dull and lifeless, something is wrong. Possibilities include:

- The certificate does not belong to the diamond you are looking at (a bait-and-switch)
- The certificate is from a laboratory that grades generously (see above)
- The certificate is fraudulent

Ask to view the diamond under standard lighting conditions (not the warm, flattering spotlights used in many jewellery stores) and compare what you see with what the certificate describes. If there is a noticeable discrepancy, do not proceed.

### Undisclosed Treatments or Enhancements

Some diamonds are treated after cutting to improve their appearance. Common treatments include:

- **Laser drilling**: Using a laser to reach dark inclusions and bleach them, improving apparent clarity
- **Fracture filling**: Injecting a glass-like substance into surface-reaching fractures to make them less visible
- **HPHT treatment**: Applying high pressure and high temperature to improve colour

These treatments are not inherently wrong, but they must be disclosed because they significantly affect value. A fracture-filled diamond is worth far less than an untreated diamond of similar appearance. Laser drill holes and fracture filling can also affect the diamond's durability.

Reputable grading laboratories note treatments on the grading report. If a jeweller does not mention treatments and the grading report does not address them, ask directly: "Has this diamond been treated or enhanced in any way?"

### Refusal to Allow Magnification

You should be able to examine any diamond under at least 10x magnification (a standard jeweller's loupe) before purchase. A jeweller who discourages or refuses this examination may be concealing inclusions, damage, or quality issues that are visible under magnification but not to the naked eye.

### Warm or Coloured Lighting

Diamonds look their best under specific lighting conditions. Warm, yellow-toned lighting makes lower-colour diamonds appear whiter. Extremely bright spotlights can mask poor cut quality by overwhelming the eye with light. Coloured ambient lighting can disguise tinted stones.

A reputable jeweller will show you a diamond under multiple lighting conditions, including daylight-equivalent lighting. If you are only shown diamonds under warm spotlights in a dimly lit room, ask to see them in natural or daylight-equivalent light. If the jeweller resists, treat this as a red flag.


## Red Flags in Ring Construction

### Very Thin Band (Under 1.5mm Width)

Fashion trends have pushed ring bands thinner over the past decade, but there are structural limits. A band thinner than 1.5mm is prone to bending, warping, and eventual cracking, particularly in softer metals like 14-karat gold. For daily wear, a minimum width of 1.8 to 2.0mm is recommended for structural integrity.

Some designs -- particularly delicate pave bands -- may be thinner by necessity. In these cases, the metal should be harder alloy and the construction should be assessed carefully. But if a standard solitaire or halo ring has a band under 1.5mm simply to reduce metal cost, this is a quality concern.

### Visible Porosity (Pitting on Metal Surface)

Porosity appears as tiny holes or pits on the metal surface, typically caused by gas bubbles trapped during casting. Minor porosity on hidden surfaces (the inside of a gallery, for example) is acceptable. Porosity on visible surfaces -- the band, the prongs, the bezel -- indicates poor casting quality or inadequate finishing. It weakens the metal and can trap dirt and bacteria.

### Uneven or Poorly Formed Prongs

Prongs should be uniform in size, shape, and position. They should make firm, even contact with the diamond and should be smoothly finished to avoid snagging on fabric. Red flags include:

- Prongs of visibly different sizes or heights
- Prongs that do not make consistent contact with the stone
- Rough or sharp prong tips
- Prongs that are excessively thick (indicating unskilled setting work) or excessively thin (indicating inadequate security)

### Rough Interior Surfaces

The inside of the band -- the surface that contacts your skin -- should be smooth and comfortable. Run your finger around the inside of the ring. If you feel roughness, bumps, casting marks, or sharp edges, the ring has not been properly finished. This is not merely an aesthetic issue; rough interior surfaces cause skin irritation and discomfort during extended wear.

### Loose Centre Stone

Before purchase, the centre stone should be secure in its setting. Gently tap the ring against your fingernail near your ear. If you hear a faint rattling or clicking, the stone is loose. A properly set diamond should not move or make sound.

### Asymmetric Setting

Hold the ring at eye level and look at it from multiple angles. The head (the part holding the diamond) should be centred on the band. The diamond should sit straight, not tilted to one side. Prongs should be evenly spaced. Halo diamonds should be uniform. Any visible asymmetry indicates careless construction.

### Sharp Edges on the Band

All edges of the band should be rounded or softened. Run your finger along every edge. If you feel sharpness, the ring has not been properly finished. Sharp edges are uncomfortable and can scratch adjacent fingers.

### Solder Marks on a "New" Ring

Visible solder joints on a ring described as new may indicate that the ring has been repaired, resized, or assembled from components that were not designed to go together. New rings manufactured as a single piece should not have visible solder marks. If you see them, ask for an explanation.


## Red Flags in Pricing

### Massive Permanent Discounts

"Was $15,000, now $7,500! Save 50%!" If a jeweller perpetually offers 40 to 60 per cent discounts, the "original" price was never the real price. It was inflated specifically to create the illusion of a deal. This practice is misleading and, in many Australian jurisdictions, may violate consumer protection law.

Compare the "discounted" price with quotes from other jewellers. You will often find that the "sale" price is comparable to or higher than regular prices elsewhere.

### No Itemised Quotes

As previously discussed, a total price without itemisation prevents you from evaluating fairness. Reputable jewellers provide transparent breakdowns as a matter of course.

### Unexplained Price Variation

If two apparently similar rings from the same jeweller have significantly different prices, the jeweller should be able to explain why. Legitimate reasons include differences in diamond specifications, metal type, design complexity, or accent stone quality. If the explanation is vague or the jeweller seems unable to justify the price difference, the pricing may be arbitrary.

### Premium Prices for Substandard Work

High price is not a guarantee of high quality. If a ring is priced at the premium level but exhibits construction red flags (thin band, rough finishing, poor prong work), the price does not reflect the quality of the product. You are paying a premium for substandard work.

### Hidden Fees

All costs should be disclosed before you commit to a purchase. Hidden fees that may appear after commitment include:

- Valuation or appraisal fees
- Engraving charges
- Ring box or packaging fees
- "Setting" fees on top of the ring cost
- Credit card surcharges not disclosed in advance
- Delivery or shipping charges

Ask explicitly: "Is this the total price, or are there any additional fees?" Get the answer in writing.

### No Return or Exchange Policy

Australian Consumer Law provides certain protections for consumers, but the specific return and exchange policies of individual jewellers vary. Before purchase, understand the jeweller's policy on:

- Returns for refund (and any restocking fees)
- Exchanges for different sizes or designs
- The timeframe for returns or exchanges
- Whether custom or bespoke pieces are returnable

A jeweller who offers no returns under any circumstances, or who has an unreasonably short return window, may lack confidence in their product.


## Red Flags in Aftercare and Warranty

### No Manufacturing Warranty

A reputable jeweller should warranty their workmanship for a minimum of 12 months, and many offer longer periods. This warranty should cover defects in manufacturing -- prong failure, stone loss due to faulty setting, structural failure of the band, or defective soldering. If a jeweller offers no warranty at all, they are not standing behind their work.

### Warranty That Excludes Normal Wear

A warranty that only covers defects "excluding normal wear and tear" can be used to deny virtually any claim. While no warranty should cover deliberate damage or misuse, a well-constructed ring should withstand normal daily wear without failure within the warranty period. If prongs break, stones fall out, or the band cracks during normal wear within the first year, this is a manufacturing defect, not normal wear.

Read the warranty terms carefully. If the exclusions are so broad that the warranty effectively covers nothing, it is not a genuine warranty.

### No Maintenance Service

A jeweller who sells engagement rings should offer ongoing maintenance services: cleaning, inspection, rhodium replating (for white gold), prong re-tipping, and resizing. If a jeweller has no capacity or willingness to service the rings they sell, they may be prioritising sales volume over customer relationships, which raises questions about overall business practice.

### Charging for Defect Repairs Under Warranty

If a ring fails due to a manufacturing defect within the warranty period, the repair should be performed at no cost to you. A jeweller who charges for warranty repairs is not honouring their warranty. This may also constitute a violation of Australian Consumer Law guarantees regarding acceptable quality.


## Pre-Purchase Checklist

Use this checklist before committing to any engagement ring purchase. Every item should receive a "yes" answer. Any "no" answers warrant further investigation or reconsideration.

### Diamond Verification

- [ ] Does the diamond come with a grading report from GIA, AGS, or IGI?
- [ ] Have you verified the certificate number matches the diamond (laser inscription on girdle, if applicable)?
- [ ] Have you viewed the diamond under magnification (minimum 10x)?
- [ ] Have you viewed the diamond under daylight-equivalent lighting, not just warm spotlights?
- [ ] Has the jeweller disclosed any treatments, enhancements, or coatings?
- [ ] If lab-grown, has the diamond been clearly identified as lab-grown on the certificate and receipt?
- [ ] Do the diamond's visual characteristics match the grading report specifications?

### Ring Construction

- [ ] Is the band at least 1.8mm wide (or, if thinner by design, is the construction appropriate)?
- [ ] Are the prongs uniform, even, and properly finished?
- [ ] Is the inside of the band smooth and free of rough surfaces?
- [ ] Is the centre stone secure with no movement or rattling?
- [ ] Is the setting symmetrical and properly centred?
- [ ] Are all edges rounded and free of sharpness?
- [ ] Is the metal surface free of visible porosity or pitting?
- [ ] If the ring includes accent stones, are they evenly set and well-matched?

### Pricing and Documentation

- [ ] Have you received an itemised quote separating diamond cost, setting cost, and labour?
- [ ] Have you compared pricing with at least two other jewellers?
- [ ] Are all fees (valuation, engraving, packaging, delivery) disclosed and included in the total?
- [ ] Is the "discount" (if any) genuine and verifiable against regular pricing?

### Policies and Warranty

- [ ] Does the jeweller provide a written manufacturing warranty?
- [ ] Do you understand the return and exchange policy, including timeframes and any restocking fees?
- [ ] Does the jeweller offer ongoing maintenance services (cleaning, inspection, re-tipping)?
- [ ] Have you received all documentation in writing (receipt, certificate, warranty, valuation)?

### Your Own Readiness

- [ ] Have you had adequate time to consider the purchase without pressure?
- [ ] Are you comfortable with the total cost, including ongoing maintenance expenses?
- [ ] Have you asked all your questions and received clear, satisfactory answers?
- [ ] Do you trust the jeweller?


## What to Do If You Spot Red Flags

### Do Not Be Pressured Into a Decision

The most important thing you can do when you encounter red flags is to slow down. Do not make a purchase while you have unresolved concerns. No legitimate business opportunity disappears because you took 48 hours to think about it.

If a jeweller becomes aggressive or dismissive when you express the desire to take time or seek a second opinion, that reaction is itself a red flag. A confident, reputable jeweller will encourage you to take whatever time you need.

### Get a Second Opinion

If something feels wrong but you are not sure, consult an independent source. Options include:

- **Independent valuer or appraiser**: A qualified valuer who does not sell jewellery can assess a diamond and ring objectively. This service typically costs $100 to $200 and can save you thousands.
- **Another jeweller**: Ask a second jeweller to review the specifications and pricing of the ring you are considering. Most will do this informally and without charge, as it is an opportunity to win your business.
- **Online gemological communities**: Forums and communities dedicated to diamond education can help you interpret grading reports and identify concerns. Be cautious of forums operated by retailers, as advice may be commercially motivated.

### Get Everything in Writing

Verbal promises are difficult to enforce. Before committing to a purchase, ensure the following are documented in writing:

- Complete diamond specifications (carat, colour, clarity, cut, certificate number)
- Metal type, karat, and weight
- Total price with itemised breakdown
- Warranty terms and duration
- Return and exchange policy
- Estimated delivery date (for custom or ordered pieces)
- Any specific promises made during the sales process (complimentary services, upgrades, guarantees)

If a jeweller is unwilling to put their promises in writing, those promises are meaningless.

### Trust Your Instincts

You do not need to be a gemologist to sense when something is wrong. If the sales process feels pushy, if answers feel evasive, if the price seems too good to be true, or if you simply feel uncomfortable, honour that feeling. There are many reputable jewellers in Australia. You do not need to purchase from one that makes you uneasy.

Walking away from a bad deal is never a mistake. The right ring, from the right jeweller, at the right price, will not require you to override your own judgment.

### Know Your Rights Under Australian Consumer Law

Under Australian Consumer Law, goods must be of acceptable quality, fit for purpose, and match their description. If an engagement ring fails to meet these guarantees -- for example, if the diamond is not as described on the receipt, or if the ring is structurally defective -- you have legal rights to a remedy regardless of the jeweller's stated policies.

The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) provides guidance on consumer rights for major purchases. If you believe you have been misled or sold a defective product, you can lodge a complaint with the ACCC or your state's fair trading body.

## Conclusion

If you have read this guide in its entirety, you now know more about engagement ring quality than the vast majority of buyers who walk into a jeweller for the first time. That knowledge is genuinely valuable, and it will serve you well regardless of where or when you decide to make your purchase.

Let us revisit the points that matter most.

The diamond is not the whole story. The ring — its construction, its materials, the precision of its setting work, the quality of its finishing — determines how that diamond is presented, protected, and experienced every single day. A ring is not simply a vehicle for a stone. It is a piece of engineering that must be beautiful, comfortable, and durable enough to withstand decades of constant wear. Evaluate the complete piece, not just the gemstone at its centre.

Cut is the most important characteristic of any diamond. More than carat weight, more than colour grade, more than clarity, it is the cut that determines whether a diamond captures light and returns it to the eye with brilliance and fire, or whether it sits flat and lifeless despite impressive numbers on a certificate. Two diamonds with the same carat weight, colour, and clarity can look profoundly different based on how well they have been cut. When your budget requires compromise — and for most buyers, it will — protect the cut above all else.

Certificates are useful tools, but they are not the final word. They provide a standardised framework for comparison, and they offer important protections against misrepresentation. But they cannot tell you how a diamond looks in person, how a setting will hold up over time, or whether a jeweller stands behind their work. Use certificates as a starting point, not a destination.

Ask questions, and pay attention to the answers. A good jeweller welcomes informed buyers. They will explain their materials, their processes, and their pricing without defensiveness. They will show you alternatives and help you understand trade-offs rather than simply steering you toward the most expensive option. If a jeweller discourages questions, treats your research as an inconvenience, or pressures you toward an immediate decision, that tells you something important about how they do business.

Take your time. There is no engagement ring emergency. The pressure to decide quickly almost always benefits the seller, not the buyer. A reputable jeweller will give you space to consider your options, compare what you have seen, and return when you are ready. The right ring will still be available — or can be made — when you have done your due diligence.

Be realistic about your budget, but do not let anyone else define it for you. Outdated rules about salary multiples are marketing inventions, not financial wisdom. Spend what you can comfortably afford, directed by the priorities this guide has helped you identify. A thoughtfully chosen ring at a moderate price point will bring more lasting satisfaction than an overextended purchase that creates financial strain at the very start of your life together.

You now understand the anatomy of a ring, the markers of quality craftsmanship, the visual impact of diamond cut, the honest trade-offs between lab-grown and natural stones, and the questions that hold any jeweller accountable. That is a solid foundation for making a decision you will be proud of for years to come.
