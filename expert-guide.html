The Engagement Ring Industry Relies on Confusion

Most buyers walk into a jeweller armed with a budget but no real understanding of what determines quality. Here’s what the industry relies on you not knowing.

Nobody Talks About the Ring Itself

Most of the industry focuses on the stone and ignores the ring. But metal alloy, casting quality, prong construction, and band thickness determine whether your ring lasts a lifetime or warps within years.

Carat Size Is Misleading

Carat measures weight, not visual size. A well-cut 0.9ct diamond can look larger than a poorly-cut 1.2ct because cut proportions determine how much light the stone reflects. Most buyers overpay chasing a number.

Clarity Grades You Can’t See

The difference between a VVS1 and VS2 diamond is invisible to the naked eye, yet the price jump can be 30–40%. Retail jewellers push higher clarity grades because the margin is better — not because you’ll notice.

Lab vs Natural Is Not a Quality Question

Lab-grown and mined diamonds are chemically, optically, and physically identical. Both are real diamonds. The choice is about values and budget, not quality. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you a story, not a stone.

What Your Jeweller Should Be Explaining

The 5 Factors That Actually Determine Ring Quality

An engagement ring is two things: a stone and a structure. Most jewellers only talk about the stone. Here’s what a master jeweller evaluates before putting their name on a piece.

1. Diamond Cut Quality

Cut is the single most important factor in how a diamond looks. It refers to how precisely the stone’s facets are angled and aligned to reflect light back to your eye.

A diamond with ideal proportions will exhibit brilliance (white light return), fire (spectral colours), and scintillation (sparkle when moved). A poorly cut diamond — even at higher carat weight — will look dull and lifeless. This is why we always prioritise cut over size.

2. Stone Setting Integrity

The setting is what holds your diamond securely for decades. A properly set stone has symmetrical prongs that grip the diamond at its girdle, with each prong applying equal pressure.

Poorly set stones sit unevenly, with prongs that are too thin, too short, or asymmetrical. Over time this leads to the stone loosening or, worst case, falling out entirely. The stone seat — the notch cut into each prong — should be precision-cut to match the exact profile of the diamond.

3. Metal Alloy Quality

Not all 18-karat gold is the same. The metals mixed with pure gold (the alloy) determine the ring’s hardness, colour consistency, and resistance to bending and scratching.

A high-quality 18k alloy uses a precise ratio of gold, palladium, silver, and copper to achieve a balanced metal that holds its shape and polishes well. Cheap alloys use excess copper, leading to brittleness, inconsistent colour, and faster tarnishing. The alloy is invisible but determines how the ring ages over decades of daily wear.

4. Casting Quality & Porosity

When a ring is cast, molten metal fills a mould. If the process is rushed or done at the wrong temperature, microscopic air bubbles get trapped inside the metal. This is called porosity.

A porous ring has internal weak points. It’s more likely to crack under pressure, warp during resizing, or develop surface pitting over time. High-quality casting requires controlled temperatures, proper vacuum conditions, and experienced finishing. You can’t see porosity on a new ring — it only reveals itself years later.

5. Band Thickness & Structural Design

A thin band may look delicate and elegant, but if it’s too thin, it will bend, warp, and lose shape with daily wear. Band thickness is a structural decision, not just an aesthetic one.

We design bands to be as slim as possible while maintaining the structural integrity needed for everyday use. The minimum safe thickness depends on the metal type, the stone size, and the setting style. A good jeweller will explain this trade-off honestly rather than giving you whatever looks thinnest in a photo.

Why This Matters

Most engagement ring buyers spend hours researching the diamond and minutes on the ring itself. But the ring is what you wear every day. The diamond sits inside it.

A $10,000 diamond in a poorly made ring is a worse investment than a $6,000 diamond in a ring built to last generations. Understanding both sides of the equation is what separates an informed buyer from an overspending one.

How to Visually Judge Ring Craftsmanship

Most buyers are never shown the craftsmanship details that determine whether a ring will last decades. The following examples illustrate what jewellers look for when assessing ring quality.

Diamond Seating

Each prong must be precisely cut so the diamond sits level inside the setting. When the seats are poorly cut, the stone may tilt slightly or rest unevenly within the prongs. This forces the metal to compensate for the misfit and can create long-term stability issues. A properly seated diamond sits level and secure, allowing the prongs to protect the stone rather than forcing it into position.

Prong Symmetry (top view)

Higher quality

Well-set diamonds have evenly spaced, symmetrical prongs that grip the stone securely at the girdle, with each prong applying equal pressure. Uneven or poorly shaped prongs create weak points in the setting, increasing the risk of the stone loosening or shifting over time. This is one of the first things an experienced jeweller checks when assessing ring quality.

Band Thickness

Lower quality

Higher quality

Extremely thin bands may look delicate in photos, but they often lack the structural strength needed for everyday wear. Over time, thin bands can bend, warp, and lose their shape — especially when supporting a heavier centre stone.

As a general guideline, a band should be no thinner than approximately 1.6mm in width and 1.6mm in thickness to maintain basic structural integrity. However, for long-term durability, most well-made engagement rings are typically crafted closer to 2.0mm in width and around 1.8mm in thickness.

A well-designed ring maintains an elegant profile while ensuring the band is strong enough to hold its shape for decades of daily wear.

Casting Quality

Higher quality

Porous casting occurs when air bubbles become trapped in the metal during the moulding process. A porous ring has hidden weak points that make it more prone to cracking, surface pitting, and problems during resizing. Dense, clean casting — achieved through controlled temperatures and proper vacuum conditions — produces a stronger, smoother result that holds up to daily wear and polishes cleanly over time.

Stone Support Structure

Higher quality

The basket — the structure beneath the diamond — is what transfers daily impact away from the stone. A weak or poorly designed basket leaves the diamond vulnerable to movement and damage over time. A reinforced basket distributes stress evenly, protects the stone from knocks, and improves the overall wearability and longevity of the ring.

Learn From Others

3 Mistakes Most Engagement Ring Buyers Make

After helping hundreds of Sydney couples, these are the patterns we see again and again.

1

Prioritising Carat Weight Over Cut Quality

The most common mistake is chasing a round number — “I want a 1-carat diamond.” But a 0.90ct diamond with an ideal cut will have more brilliance, more fire, and appear larger face-up than a 1.10ct stone with a deep or shallow cut.

What to do instead: Start with cut quality (Excellent or Ideal grade), then find the largest stone within your budget at that cut standard. You’ll end up with a more beautiful diamond and often save 15–25%.

2

Overpaying for Invisible Colour and Clarity Grades

Many buyers assume the highest colour and clarity grades automatically mean a better-looking diamond. In reality, once a diamond is set in a ring, the difference between D colour and E–F colour, or VVS clarity and VS clarity, is rarely visible to the naked eye. Most of the premium is tied to microscopic differences only visible under magnification.

What to do instead: Choose a diamond around E–F colour and VS clarity, where visual quality is already exceptional. Redirect the savings toward a better cut, a larger stone, or a higher-quality ring setting. Your diamond will look the same — but your ring will be better.

3

Ignoring Ring Construction Entirely

Most buyers focus entirely on the diamond and never ask about the ring. They don’t check band thickness, prong construction, or casting quality. The result is a beautiful stone in a ring that bends, loosens, or needs constant repair.

What to do instead: Ask your jeweller about the metal alloy they use, the minimum band thickness for your design, and how the prongs are constructed. A good jeweller will welcome these questions. If they can’t answer them, that tells you something.

What I Tell Clients in Private Consultations

These are the things I find myself explaining in almost every first consultation. They’re worth knowing before you walk into any jeweller.

The diamond is only part of the ring

A beautiful stone means very little if the ring holding it isn’t built to last. The setting, prong construction, band thickness, and overall structural design determine whether your ring holds up to decades of daily wear — or needs constant repair. Durability is craftsmanship, and craftsmanship is worth asking about.

Cut quality matters more than carat size

The way a diamond is cut determines how much light it returns to your eye. That’s what creates brilliance, fire, and sparkle. A smaller diamond with an excellent cut will consistently outperform a larger one with a mediocre cut. If you only optimise for one thing, make it cut.

Colour and clarity grades are often overemphasised

Many buyers are encouraged to pay large premiums for extremely high grades like D colour or VVS clarity. In reality, a well-cut E or F colour diamond with VS clarity will appear virtually identical to the naked eye once set in a ring. The goal is to prioritise what actually affects beauty — not to overpay for differences that are only visible under magnification.

Retail pricing often reflects overhead

When you buy from a large retailer, a significant portion of the price covers their shopfront, staffing, and marketing — not the ring itself. That’s not dishonest, but it’s worth understanding. A private jeweller with lower overheads can often deliver the same or better quality at a meaningfully different price point.
